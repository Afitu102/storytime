🐟 Girl Who Caught Impossible Fishes

🌍 African Folktales • 🕒 25 min read

🐟 Chapter One – The Girl With the Lucky Net

The morning sun was just coming up over the village of Kitala.

The lake was calm.

Small waves touched the wooden canoes near the shore.

Birds flew low over the water.

And among the fishermen was a young girl named Amara.

Amara was known for one thing.

She could catch fish.

Not just one or two.

Sometimes, her small basket was fuller than those of grown fishermen.

The older fishermen often laughed about it.

"That girl must have a secret."

Amara would only smile.

"Maybe the lake likes me."

Her grandmother, Nkiru, always warned her not to become too proud.

"Luck is like a bird."

"It can sit on your shoulder today and fly away tomorrow."

Amara remembered those words every morning.

That day, she carried her small fishing net toward the shore.

Her younger cousin, Zuberi, followed behind her.

"Wait for me!"

Amara turned.

"You are too slow."

Zuberi laughed.

"Because you have long legs."

Amara laughed too.

They reached the lake.

Their mother had given them one basket for the morning catch.

If they caught enough fish, there would be food for the whole family that evening.

Amara stepped into the shallow water.

She spread her net.

She waited.

Nothing.

She moved farther.

Still nothing.

Zuberi looked worried.

"Maybe Grandma was right."

Amara smiled.

"About what?"

"Maybe your luck flew away."

Amara shook her head.

"Then we will call it back."

She lifted the net again.

She threw it toward a quiet part of the lake.

The net disappeared beneath the water.

Amara waited.

Then she felt something pull.

Her eyes widened.

"Zuberi!"

He grabbed the rope.

They pulled together.

The net came out of the water.

Fish jumped everywhere.

Zuberi screamed with excitement.

"We caught them!"

Amara laughed.

The basket quickly filled.

But then something strange happened.

Amara noticed one fish lying at the bottom of the net.

It was different from the others.

Its scales were bright gold.

It was still alive.

It looked directly at her.

Amara stopped smiling.

"Zuberi."

He came closer.

"What?"

Amara pointed at the fish.

Zuberi's mouth opened.

"I have never seen a fish like that."

The golden fish moved its tail.

Then it made a strange sound.

Not the sound of a fish.

It sounded almost like a whisper.

Amara leaned closer.

"What did you say?"

The fish moved again.

Amara heard the whisper clearly this time.

"Take me home."

Amara jumped backward.

Zuberi stared at her.

"What happened?"

Amara pointed at the fish.

"It spoke."

Zuberi laughed.

"Fish don't speak."

Amara looked at the golden fish again.

It was staring at her.

Then it whispered again.

"Take me home."

Zuberi stopped laughing.

They looked at each other.

Neither child touched the fish.

A moment later, Amara carefully placed it inside a smaller basket.

She covered the basket with a cloth.

Zuberi looked nervous.

"Shouldn't we throw it back?"

Amara shook her head.

"I don't know."

They started walking home.

People noticed the basket immediately.

The catch was much bigger than anything Amara had brought home before.

Her mother, Sade, came running out of the house.

"Amara!"

She looked inside the basket.

Her face filled with happiness.

"Look at all these fish!"

Neighbors gathered around.

Everyone celebrated.

Children danced.

The fishermen smiled.

For the first time in many weeks, Amara's family had enough food to share with others.

Her grandmother Nkiru came outside.

She looked at the fish.

Then she looked at Amara.

"Where did you catch these?"

Amara pointed toward the lake.

"Near the old fig tree."

Nkiru's smile disappeared.

"Did you catch anything else?"

Amara became quiet.

She looked at the covered basket.

Nkiru noticed.

"What's inside?"

Amara slowly removed the cloth.

The golden fish was still there.

Nkiru froze.

Nobody spoke.

Then the old woman whispered:

"Where did you find this?"

Amara answered:

"In my net."

Nkiru took one step backward.

"Put it back."

Amara looked confused.

"Grandma, why?"

Nkiru looked toward the lake.

"Because some gifts bring happiness."

"And some gifts ask for something in return."

Before Amara could answer, the golden fish moved.

Its mouth opened.

Everyone heard the whisper.

"Your family will never be hungry again."

The people around the house gasped.

Sade covered her mouth.

Zuberi stepped behind Amara.

But Nkiru did not look surprised.

She looked frightened.

"Amara."

Amara looked at her grandmother.

"Yes?"

Nkiru pointed toward the lake.

"Take that fish back before sunset."

Amara looked at the golden fish.

"But it gave us luck."

Nkiru shook her head.

"No."

She looked deeply into Amara's eyes.

"It gave you a promise."

Amara's smile disappeared.

Then the golden fish whispered one final thing.

"Tomorrow, bring your net alone."

Amara stared at it.

She suddenly realized something.

The fish had not asked her to return it.

It had asked her to come back.

Alone.

🌙 End of Chapter One

🌅 Chapter Two – The Fish That Knew Her Name

Amara did not sleep well that night.

She kept thinking about the golden fish.

She kept hearing its voice.

"Tomorrow, bring your net alone."

Before sunrise, Amara got out of bed.

She looked outside.

The village was still quiet.

Her mother was sleeping.

Zuberi was sleeping too.

Amara picked up her net.

She wanted to leave quietly.

But someone was already sitting outside.

It was Grandma Nkiru.

"You are going to the lake."

Amara stopped.

"Yes, Grandma."

Nkiru looked at the net.

"Did I not tell you to take the fish back?"

Amara nodded.

"I did."

"But the fish told me to come back alone."

Nkiru became silent.

She slowly stood.

"Then you must not go."

Amara frowned.

"But why?"

Nkiru looked toward the lake.

"Because a thing that knows your name before you tell it is not an ordinary thing."

Amara looked confused.

"The fish knows my name?"

Nkiru nodded.

"It called you Amara."

Amara's face changed.

She had not told the fish her name.

Nkiru held her hand.

"Listen to me."

"Luck is good."

"Happiness is good."

"But when something gives too much, ask why."

Amara looked at her net.

"What if the fish only wants to help us?"

Nkiru smiled sadly.

"Then it will still help us after you leave it alone."

Amara thought about it.

Then she heard something.

A splash.

Both of them turned toward the lake.

Another splash followed.

Then another.

Fish were jumping close to the shore.

Many fish.

More than Amara had ever seen.

Nkiru's face became serious.

"Go inside."

Amara did not move.

The fish suddenly stopped jumping.

The lake became still.

Then the golden fish appeared near the shore.

It lifted its head above the water.

Amara heard its voice.

"Amara."

Nkiru stepped in front of her.

"Go away."

The fish did not move.

"I came because she called me."

Amara looked at her grandmother.

"I didn't call it."

Nkiru looked frightened.

"Exactly."

The fish disappeared beneath the water.

Amara watched the place where it had vanished.

A few moments later, something floated toward them.

It was a small wooden box.

It reached the shore and stopped at Amara's feet.

She bent down.

Nkiru grabbed her hand.

"Don't touch it."

Amara looked at the box.

There was a symbol carved into the lid.

Nkiru recognized it.

"Where did you see that?"

Amara shook her head.

"I don't know."

Nkiru's eyes filled with worry.

"I have seen it before."

She looked at the lake.

"Long before you were born."

Amara stared at her.

"Grandma, what is inside?"

Nkiru did not answer.

The wooden box suddenly opened by itself.

Inside was a small piece of blue cloth.

Amara picked it up.

Something was written on it.

ONE NET.

Under it were two more words.

ONE WISH.

Amara looked at her grandmother.

"What does it mean?"

Nkiru sat down slowly.

"It means the fish is offering you a choice."

"What choice?"

Nkiru looked toward the water.

"Ask for anything."

Amara's eyes widened.

"Anything?"

Nkiru nodded.

"Anything your heart wants."

Amara thought about her family.

She thought about food.

She thought about the smiles she had seen the day before.

She thought about her mother laughing with the neighbors.

Then she remembered Nkiru's warning.

"Some gifts ask for something in return."

Amara looked at the box again.

"What happens if I make a wish?"

Nkiru lowered her voice.

"That is what scares me."

A voice came from the lake.

"You will never regret it."

Amara turned.

The golden fish was there again.

This time, it was much closer.

"Choose happiness."

Amara looked at the fish.

"Why do you want me to choose?"

The fish smiled.

Amara could have sworn it smiled.

"Because you are the only one who can."

Nkiru stood quickly.

"Amara, don't answer it."

But Amara had already made up her mind.

She picked up the wooden box.

"I want to ask one question."

The fish went still.

"Ask."

Amara looked directly at it.

"Why did you choose me?"

The fish did not answer.

The water around it began to move.

The lake rose slightly.

Then the fish whispered:

"Because your father once made a wish."

Amara froze.

Her father had died when she was very young.

She had never heard of him making any wish.

"What did he wish for?"

The fish sank lower.

"He wished for a child."

Amara's heart began beating faster.

"Then why did you choose me?"

The fish looked at her.

"Because he promised you to the lake."

Nkiru gasped.

Amara stepped backward.

"No."

The fish disappeared.

The lake became calm again.

Amara looked at Nkiru.

"Is that true?"

Nkiru could not answer.

Amara's eyes filled with tears.

"Grandma."

Nkiru finally spoke.

"Your father made that wish before you were born."

Amara stared at her.

"And you knew?"

Nkiru nodded.

"Your mother knew too."

Amara dropped the blue cloth.

Her happiness from the previous day suddenly felt far away.

Then she heard footsteps behind them.

Her mother was standing there.

She had heard everything.

"Amara..."

Amara looked at her.

"Did Father promise me to the lake?"

Her mother began to cry.

She nodded.

Amara stepped back.

Then something moved inside the wooden box.

A second piece of cloth slowly came out.

This one was red.

There were only three words written on it.

THE DEBT IS DUE.

Amara looked toward the lake.

The golden fish appeared one last time.

"Tomorrow, bring your net."

The fish disappeared.

Amara looked at her mother.

Then at her grandmother.

"What happens tomorrow?"

Neither woman answered.

And for the first time, Amara understood that the fish had never brought her luck.

It had only come to collect it.

🌙 End of Chapter Two

🌊 Chapter Three – The Catch Nobody Could Explain

Amara did not go back to the lake that morning.

She stayed inside the house.

She was angry.

She was scared too.

Most of all, she wanted to know the truth about her father.

Her mother, Sade, sat beside her.

Grandma Nkiru stood near the doorway.

"Tell me everything."

Sade looked at her mother.

Nkiru gave a slow nod.

Sade took a deep breath.

"Your father was a very lucky fisherman."

Amara looked at her.

"Like me?"

Her mother nodded.

"Like you."

Amara waited.

"He could throw one net and come home with enough fish for many families."

Nkiru added:

"People thought he was blessed."

Amara looked down.

"But he wanted more."

Her mother nodded.

"He wanted a child."

Amara became quiet.

"And the fish gave him one?"

Nkiru shook her head.

"Not exactly."

Sade wiped her eyes.

"One evening, your father went fishing alone."

The sun was already going down.

He threw his net once.

The net came back empty.

He threw it again.

Nothing.

Then he threw it a third time.

That was when he caught the golden fish.

Amara's eyes widened.

"The same fish?"

Nkiru nodded.

"The same fish."

Amara stood up.

"What did Father do?"

Sade looked at the floor.

"He made a wish."

Amara waited.

"He asked for a child."

A long silence filled the room.

Then Amara asked:

"What did the fish ask for?"

Nkiru answered.

"Nothing."

Amara frowned.

"Nothing?"

Nkiru shook her head.

"That was the problem."

Amara slowly sat down.

"Then why is there a debt?"

Sade looked at her.

"Because your father promised to pay later."

Amara's heart sank.

"Pay with what?"

Nobody answered.

Amara already knew.

She whispered:

"Me."

Sade began crying.

Amara walked outside.

Zuberi was waiting near the cooking place.

"Where are you going?"

Amara looked toward the lake.

"To find the truth."

Zuberi stood up.

"I'm coming."

Amara shook her head.

"No."

Zuberi frowned.

"You said the fish wanted you alone."

Amara looked at him.

"That's why you must stay here."

She picked up her net.

Zuberi grabbed her hand.

"Come back."

Amara smiled.

"I will."

She walked toward the lake.

This time, she was not going because she wanted fish.

She wanted answers.

When she reached the shore, the water was strangely quiet.

No birds.

No boats.

No fishermen.

Amara stepped into the water.

She threw her net.

It sank.

She pulled it back.

There was one fish inside.

It was not golden.

It was small and silver.

Amara threw it back.

She cast the net again.

Another fish.

She threw it back.

Again.

Again.

Each time, she caught exactly one fish.

Then the net became heavy.

Very heavy.

Amara pulled with both hands.

She finally dragged it onto the shore.

The net was full.

But there was something else inside.

A small wooden paddle.

Amara picked it up.

Her father's name was carved into it.

JABARI.

Amara's hands began to shake.

"Father..."

The water moved behind her.

She turned.

The golden fish was floating near the shore.

"You found his paddle."

Amara stared at it.

"Where did you get it?"

The fish moved closer.

"Where he left it."

Amara looked around.

"Where?"

The fish pointed with its tail toward the far side of the lake.

"Beyond the old rocks."

Amara tightened her grip on the paddle.

"Why did Father leave it there?"

The fish did not answer.

Instead, it said:

"Ask him."

Amara froze.

"My father is dead."

The golden fish sank halfway beneath the water.

"Not everything that disappears is dead."

Amara's heart began racing.

"What are you saying?"

The fish turned away.

"Come tomorrow."

Amara stepped forward.

"No."

The fish stopped.

"You said you would tell me the truth."

The fish slowly turned around.

"Then ask the lake."

The water suddenly became dark.

A deep sound came from beneath it.

Amara stepped back.

The golden fish disappeared.

The lake became calm again.

Amara looked at the paddle.

Then she noticed something carved underneath her father's name.

A date.

The same date her father was said to have died.

But below the date were three fresh words.

HE IS WAITING.

Amara dropped the paddle.

A voice suddenly came from the water.

"Amara."

She looked around.

Nobody was there.

The voice came again.

"Don't make the wish."

Amara knew that voice.

She had heard it many times in old memories.

It was her father's voice.

She moved closer to the water.

"Father?"

The lake answered.

"Your father is not the one you should fear."

Amara's eyes widened.

"Then who should I fear?"

The water went silent.

Then a shadow moved beneath the surface.

It was moving toward her.

Amara grabbed her net.

The shadow stopped at her feet.

Something touched the bottom of the net.

She pulled it up.

There was a small clay jar inside.

Her father's name was written on it.

Amara opened the jar.

Inside was a folded piece of cloth.

She opened it.

Only one sentence was written there.

"Your mother knows why I never came home."

Amara's hands went cold.

She turned toward the village.

She had to go home.

But this time, she was not going home to ask her mother about the fish.

She was going to ask about her father.

And she already knew her mother was hiding something.

🌙 End of Chapter Three

🌙 Chapter Four – The Secret Beneath the Canoe

Amara reached home before the sun went down.

She kept the clay jar hidden beneath her wrapper.

Her mother was outside, sorting the fish from the morning catch.

Zuberi was helping her.

Grandma Nkiru sat near the doorway.

She looked at Amara's face.

"You found something."

Amara nodded.

She brought out the clay jar.

Her mother dropped the fish in her hand.

Sade stared at the jar.

"Where did you get that?"

Amara placed the folded cloth on the table.

"The lake gave it to me."

Sade picked up the cloth.

She read the words.

Her face changed.

"Who told you this?"

Amara looked at her.

"Father."

Sade stepped backward.

Zuberi stopped sorting the fish.

"Uncle Jabari?"

Amara nodded.

Sade covered her face.

Nkiru slowly stood.

"It is time."

Sade looked at her mother.

"Mother..."

Nkiru shook her head.

"No more hiding."

Amara looked between them.

"Tell me what happened to Father."

Sade sat down.

She was quiet for a long time.

Then she spoke.

"Your father did not die that night."

Amara froze.

"What?"

Sade wiped her tears.

"He went to the lake."

"He never came back."

Amara shook her head.

"Then why did everyone say he died?"

Nkiru answered.

"Because the village needed an answer."

Amara looked at her.

"And the truth?"

Nkiru pointed at the clay jar.

"The truth stayed with the lake."

Amara became angry.

"You all knew!"

Sade started crying.

"We wanted to protect you."

"From what?"

Nobody answered.

Then Nkiru walked toward the old storage hut.

She returned carrying a small wooden key.

"Your father left this with me."

Amara took it.

She looked at the key.

"What does it open?"

Nkiru looked toward the lake.

"His canoe."

Amara's heart jumped.

"His canoe is still here?"

Nkiru nodded.

"Under the old shed."

Amara grabbed a lantern.

Zuberi followed her.

Sade tried to stop them.

"Amara, wait."

Amara turned.

"I have waited my whole life."

She walked toward the shed.

The canoe was covered with an old brown cloth.

Amara pulled it away.

The wood was old.

But one thing caught her eye.

A fresh golden mark was carved into the side.

The same mark from the wooden box.

Amara inserted the key.

A small compartment opened beneath the seat.

Inside was an old fishing knife.

There was also a small leather pouch.

Amara opened it.

A few dried leaves fell out.

Then she found a folded letter.

She opened it carefully.

"Sade, if you are reading this, I did not return."

Amara's hands began to shake.

She continued reading.

"I made a mistake."

"I thought the golden fish had given me a blessing."

"It was not a blessing."

Amara looked at her mother.

Sade was crying.

Amara continued.

"The fish gave me what I wanted."

"But every gift has a price."

The letter continued.

"If I disappear, never let Amara make another wish."

Amara stopped reading.

Her name was written there.

She turned the page.

There was one final line.

"If the fish returns, it means the price has come due."

The lantern suddenly went out.

Zuberi grabbed Amara's arm.

"Amara."

She looked outside.

The lake was glowing.

A golden light moved across the water.

Slowly.

Closer.

Closer.

Then the golden fish appeared.

But this time it was not alone.

Behind it were hundreds of fish.

All swimming toward the shore.

Sade whispered:

"It's happening again."

Amara looked at her.

"What happened before?"

Sade did not answer.

The golden fish opened its mouth.

"Bring the net."

Amara stood still.

The fish spoke again.

"Bring the net, Amara."

Nkiru grabbed her shoulder.

"Do not go."

Amara looked at the lake.

The fish were gathering closer.

Then something appeared behind them.

A small wooden canoe.

It was moving toward the shore.

Nobody was inside.

Amara stared at it.

The canoe touched the sand.

A man's hand slowly appeared over its side.

Amara stopped breathing.

The hand was holding an old fishing net.

She knew that net.

It belonged to her father.

Then a voice came from inside the canoe.

"Amara."

She stepped forward.

Her mother screamed.

"Don't!"

Amara stopped.

The voice came again.

"Do not trust the fish."

Amara stared into the canoe.

A man's face slowly appeared.

Older.

Tired.

But unmistakable.

It was her father.

Amara's eyes filled with tears.

"Father?"

He looked at her.

"I have been waiting for you."

The golden fish suddenly struck the water with its tail.

The lake rose behind the canoe.

Jabari looked frightened.

"Run!"

Amara stepped back.

The water rushed toward them.

And the golden fish whispered:

"Your father belongs to us."

Amara grabbed the net.

She knew she could not run anymore.

If she wanted her father back,

she would have to face the thing that had taken him.

🌙 End of Chapter Four

✨ Chapter Five – The Price of the Golden Fish

Amara stood at the edge of the lake.

Her father's face was still inside the old canoe.

She could hardly believe what she was seeing.

Jabari looked older than she remembered.

His hair was grey.

His face was tired.

But his eyes were the same.

"Father..."

Jabari reached toward her.

"Come closer."

Amara wanted to run to him.

But Grandma Nkiru held her back.

"Don't go."

Amara looked at her.

"That is my father."

Nkiru shook her head.

"Yes."

"But something is holding him there."

The golden fish moved closer to the shore.

"Give us the girl."

Sade stepped in front of Amara.

"No."

The fish struck the water.

A strong wave hit the shore.

Everyone stepped back.

Jabari shouted from the canoe.

"Amara, listen to me!"

She looked at him.

"Do not make a wish."

The golden fish laughed.

It was a strange sound.

It did not sound like a fish anymore.

"She already has."

Amara shook her head.

"I never made a wish."

The fish stared at her.

"You wished for your father."

Amara became silent.

She had wished for that many times.

When she was little.

Before sleeping.

Whenever she looked at other children with their fathers.

She had always wished that one day her father would come home.

The golden fish smiled.

"Now you understand."

Amara looked at Jabari.

"How do I free you?"

Jabari looked at the net in her hands.

"Catch the golden fish."

Nkiru gasped.

"Jabari!"

He looked at her.

"There is no other way."

Amara looked at the net.

Then she looked at the golden fish.

"If I catch you, my father goes free?"

The fish moved in a slow circle.

"If you can catch me."

Amara stepped into the water.

Sade grabbed her arm.

"Amara, don't!"

Amara looked at her mother.

"I have to."

She walked farther.

The water reached her knees.

The golden fish moved away.

Amara raised the net.

She threw it.

The net landed perfectly.

But when she pulled it back,

the golden fish was gone.

Only empty water remained.

A voice came from behind her.

"Too slow."

Amara turned.

The fish was behind her.

She threw the net again.

Nothing.

Again.

Nothing.

Again.

The fish escaped every time.

Jabari shouted.

"Don't chase it!"

Amara stopped.

She understood.

The fish wanted her to chase it.

She lowered the net.

Then she stood still.

The lake became quiet.

The golden fish slowly came toward her.

It was curious.

Amara waited.

Closer.

Closer.

The fish came within reach.

Amara suddenly lifted the net.

She threw it.

The golden fish tried to escape.

But the net closed around it.

Amara pulled.

The fish fought hard.

The water began shaking.

The old canoe rocked.

Jabari grabbed the side.

"Hold on!"

Amara pulled with all her strength.

The net came closer.

The golden fish was trapped.

Suddenly, the lake became completely still.

The fish stopped moving.

Its golden scales began to fade.

Amara stared.

The fish became silver.

Then grey.

Then it became an ordinary fish.

The old canoe suddenly moved toward the shore.

Jabari jumped out.

His feet touched the sand.

He was free.

Amara ran toward him.

They held each other tightly.

Sade ran after them.

Nkiru followed.

For the first time in many years,

the whole family stood together.

Amara was crying.

"I thought you were dead."

Jabari held her face.

"I wanted to come home."

"Every day."

Amara looked toward the lake.

The water was calm again.

"Why did the fish keep you?"

Jabari looked at Nkiru.

Then he looked at Sade.

"Because I wanted too much."

Amara frowned.

"Too much?"

Jabari nodded.

"I wanted wealth."

"I wanted a child."

"I wanted every day to be better than the one before."

He looked at the lake.

"And I forgot to be thankful for what I already had."

Amara looked at the fish inside the net.

It was still alive.

She slowly opened the net.

The fish swam away.

She watched it disappear.

Zuberi ran toward them from the village.

"Amara!"

He stopped when he saw Jabari.

His eyes became wide.

"Uncle?"

Jabari smiled.

"Come here, boy."

Zuberi ran into his arms.

Everyone laughed.

For the first time, the laughter felt free.

There was no strange promise.

No golden fish.

No hidden price.

Only happiness.

That evening, the whole village gathered around Amara's family.

There was food.

There was music.

There was dancing.

The fish caught that morning fed everyone.

Jabari watched Amara smile.

"That is the luck I should have wanted."

Amara looked at him.

"What luck?"

He smiled.

"Having people to share happiness with."

Grandma Nkiru nodded.

"Now you understand."

Amara smiled.

She looked toward the lake one final time.

Far away, something golden flashed beneath the water.

Amara did not chase it.

She simply smiled.

Then she turned back to her family.

🌿 Moral of the Story

True luck is not getting everything you want.
