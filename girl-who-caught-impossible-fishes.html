🐟 Girl Who Caught Impossible Fishes

🌍 African Folktales • 🕒 25 min read

🐟 Chapter One – The Girl With the Lucky Net

The morning sun was just coming up over the village of Kitala.

The lake was calm.

Small waves touched the wooden canoes near the shore.

Birds flew low over the water.

And among the fishermen was a young girl named Amara.

Amara was known for one thing.

She could catch fish.

Not just one or two.

Sometimes, her small basket was fuller than those of grown fishermen.

The older fishermen often laughed about it.

"That girl must have a secret."

Amara would only smile.

"Maybe the lake likes me."

Her grandmother, Nkiru, always warned her not to become too proud.

"Luck is like a bird."

"It can sit on your shoulder today and fly away tomorrow."

Amara remembered those words every morning.

That day, she carried her small fishing net toward the shore.

Her younger cousin, Zuberi, followed behind her.

"Wait for me!"

Amara turned.

"You are too slow."

Zuberi laughed.

"Because you have long legs."

Amara laughed too.

They reached the lake.

Their mother had given them one basket for the morning catch.

If they caught enough fish, there would be food for the whole family that evening.

Amara stepped into the shallow water.

She spread her net.

She waited.

Nothing.

She moved farther.

Still nothing.

Zuberi looked worried.

"Maybe Grandma was right."

Amara smiled.

"About what?"

"Maybe your luck flew away."

Amara shook her head.

"Then we will call it back."

She lifted the net again.

She threw it toward a quiet part of the lake.

The net disappeared beneath the water.

Amara waited.

Then she felt something pull.

Her eyes widened.

"Zuberi!"

He grabbed the rope.

They pulled together.

The net came out of the water.

Fish jumped everywhere.

Zuberi screamed with excitement.

"We caught them!"

Amara laughed.

The basket quickly filled.

But then something strange happened.

Amara noticed one fish lying at the bottom of the net.

It was different from the others.

Its scales were bright gold.

It was still alive.

It looked directly at her.

Amara stopped smiling.

"Zuberi."

He came closer.

"What?"

Amara pointed at the fish.

Zuberi's mouth opened.

"I have never seen a fish like that."

The golden fish moved its tail.

Then it made a strange sound.

Not the sound of a fish.

It sounded almost like a whisper.

Amara leaned closer.

"What did you say?"

The fish moved again.

Amara heard the whisper clearly this time.

"Take me home."

Amara jumped backward.

Zuberi stared at her.

"What happened?"

Amara pointed at the fish.

"It spoke."

Zuberi laughed.

"Fish don't speak."

Amara looked at the golden fish again.

It was staring at her.

Then it whispered again.

"Take me home."

Zuberi stopped laughing.

They looked at each other.

Neither child touched the fish.

A moment later, Amara carefully placed it inside a smaller basket.

She covered the basket with a cloth.

Zuberi looked nervous.

"Shouldn't we throw it back?"

Amara shook her head.

"I don't know."

They started walking home.

People noticed the basket immediately.

The catch was much bigger than anything Amara had brought home before.

Her mother, Sade, came running out of the house.

"Amara!"

She looked inside the basket.

Her face filled with happiness.

"Look at all these fish!"

Neighbors gathered around.

Everyone celebrated.

Children danced.

The fishermen smiled.

For the first time in many weeks, Amara's family had enough food to share with others.

Her grandmother Nkiru came outside.

She looked at the fish.

Then she looked at Amara.

"Where did you catch these?"

Amara pointed toward the lake.

"Near the old fig tree."

Nkiru's smile disappeared.

"Did you catch anything else?"

Amara became quiet.

She looked at the covered basket.

Nkiru noticed.

"What's inside?"

Amara slowly removed the cloth.

The golden fish was still there.

Nkiru froze.

Nobody spoke.

Then the old woman whispered:

"Where did you find this?"

Amara answered:

"In my net."

Nkiru took one step backward.

"Put it back."

Amara looked confused.

"Grandma, why?"

Nkiru looked toward the lake.

"Because some gifts bring happiness."

"And some gifts ask for something in return."

Before Amara could answer, the golden fish moved.

Its mouth opened.

Everyone heard the whisper.

"Your family will never be hungry again."

The people around the house gasped.

Sade covered her mouth.

Zuberi stepped behind Amara.

But Nkiru did not look surprised.

She looked frightened.

"Amara."

Amara looked at her grandmother.

"Yes?"

Nkiru pointed toward the lake.

"Take that fish back before sunset."

Amara looked at the golden fish.

"But it gave us luck."

Nkiru shook her head.

"No."

She looked deeply into Amara's eyes.

"It gave you a promise."

Amara's smile disappeared.

Then the golden fish whispered one final thing.

"Tomorrow, bring your net alone."

Amara stared at it.

She suddenly realized something.

The fish had not asked her to return it.

It had asked her to come back.

Alone.

🌙 End of Chapter One

🌅 Chapter Two – The Fish That Knew Her Name

Amara did not sleep well that night.

She kept thinking about the golden fish.

She kept hearing its voice.

"Tomorrow, bring your net alone."

Before sunrise, Amara got out of bed.

She looked outside.

The village was still quiet.

Her mother was sleeping.

Zuberi was sleeping too.

Amara picked up her net.

She wanted to leave quietly.

But someone was already sitting outside.

It was Grandma Nkiru.

"You are going to the lake."

Amara stopped.

"Yes, Grandma."

Nkiru looked at the net.

"Did I not tell you to take the fish back?"

Amara nodded.

"I did."

"But the fish told me to come back alone."

Nkiru became silent.

She slowly stood.

"Then you must not go."

Amara frowned.

"But why?"

Nkiru looked toward the lake.

"Because a thing that knows your name before you tell it is not an ordinary thing."

Amara looked confused.

"The fish knows my name?"

Nkiru nodded.

"It called you Amara."

Amara's face changed.

She had not told the fish her name.

Nkiru held her hand.

"Listen to me."

"Luck is good."

"Happiness is good."

"But when something gives too much, ask why."

Amara looked at her net.

"What if the fish only wants to help us?"

Nkiru smiled sadly.

"Then it will still help us after you leave it alone."

Amara thought about it.

Then she heard something.

A splash.

Both of them turned toward the lake.

Another splash followed.

Then another.

Fish were jumping close to the shore.

Many fish.

More than Amara had ever seen.

Nkiru's face became serious.

"Go inside."

Amara did not move.

The fish suddenly stopped jumping.

The lake became still.

Then the golden fish appeared near the shore.

It lifted its head above the water.

Amara heard its voice.

"Amara."

Nkiru stepped in front of her.

"Go away."

The fish did not move.

"I came because she called me."

Amara looked at her grandmother.

"I didn't call it."

Nkiru looked frightened.

"Exactly."

The fish disappeared beneath the water.

Amara watched the place where it had vanished.

A few moments later, something floated toward them.

It was a small wooden box.

It reached the shore and stopped at Amara's feet.

She bent down.

Nkiru grabbed her hand.

"Don't touch it."

Amara looked at the box.

There was a symbol carved into the lid.

Nkiru recognized it.

"Where did you see that?"

Amara shook her head.

"I don't know."

Nkiru's eyes filled with worry.

"I have seen it before."

She looked at the lake.

"Long before you were born."

Amara stared at her.

"Grandma, what is inside?"

Nkiru did not answer.

The wooden box suddenly opened by itself.

Inside was a small piece of blue cloth.

Amara picked it up.

Something was written on it.

ONE NET.

Under it were two more words.

ONE WISH.

Amara looked at her grandmother.

"What does it mean?"

Nkiru sat down slowly.

"It means the fish is offering you a choice."

"What choice?"

Nkiru looked toward the water.

"Ask for anything."

Amara's eyes widened.

"Anything?"

Nkiru nodded.

"Anything your heart wants."

Amara thought about her family.

She thought about food.

She thought about the smiles she had seen the day before.

She thought about her mother laughing with the neighbors.

Then she remembered Nkiru's warning.

"Some gifts ask for something in return."

Amara looked at the box again.

"What happens if I make a wish?"

Nkiru lowered her voice.

"That is what scares me."

A voice came from the lake.

"You will never regret it."

Amara turned.

The golden fish was there again.

This time, it was much closer.

"Choose happiness."

Amara looked at the fish.

"Why do you want me to choose?"

The fish smiled.

Amara could have sworn it smiled.

"Because you are the only one who can."

Nkiru stood quickly.

"Amara, don't answer it."

But Amara had already made up her mind.

She picked up the wooden box.

"I want to ask one question."

The fish went still.

"Ask."

Amara looked directly at it.

"Why did you choose me?"

The fish did not answer.

The water around it began to move.

The lake rose slightly.

Then the fish whispered:

"Because your father once made a wish."

Amara froze.

Her father had died when she was very young.

She had never heard of him making any wish.

"What did he wish for?"

The fish sank lower.

"He wished for a child."

Amara's heart began beating faster.

"Then why did you choose me?"

The fish looked at her.

"Because he promised you to the lake."

Nkiru gasped.

Amara stepped backward.

"No."

The fish disappeared.

The lake became calm again.

Amara looked at Nkiru.

"Is that true?"

Nkiru could not answer.

Amara's eyes filled with tears.

"Grandma."

Nkiru finally spoke.

"Your father made that wish before you were born."

Amara stared at her.

"And you knew?"

Nkiru nodded.

"Your mother knew too."

Amara dropped the blue cloth.

Her happiness from the previous day suddenly felt far away.

Then she heard footsteps behind them.

Her mother was standing there.

She had heard everything.

"Amara..."

Amara looked at her.

"Did Father promise me to the lake?"

Her mother began to cry.

She nodded.

Amara stepped back.

Then something moved inside the wooden box.

A second piece of cloth slowly came out.

This one was red.

There were only three words written on it.

THE DEBT IS DUE.

Amara looked toward the lake.

The golden fish appeared one last time.

"Tomorrow, bring your net."

The fish disappeared.

Amara looked at her mother.

Then at her grandmother.

"What happens tomorrow?"

Neither woman answered.

And for the first time, Amara understood that the fish had never brought her luck.

It had only come to collect it.
