🔥 Tales from Grandma's Fire

🌍 African Folktales • 🕒 30 min read

🔥 Chapter One – When Grandma Stopped the Story

The fire was almost going out.

Only a few red pieces of wood were still glowing.

Grandma Abeni pushed one of them closer with a small stick.

The fire jumped.

A few sparks flew into the dark.

Beside her, a young girl named Sewa pulled her wrapper tighter around her shoulders.

She had been waiting for this moment all evening.

Grandma always told stories at night.

But tonight was different.

Grandma had promised to tell her the story of a village that once disappeared before sunrise.

Sewa leaned closer.

"Grandma, you said this story happened for real."

Grandma looked at her.

"I said it happened."

Sewa smiled.

"That means it is real."

Grandma shook her head slowly.

"Not everything that is real should be repeated."

Sewa's smile disappeared.

"Why?"

Grandma looked into the fire.

"Because some stories remember the people who tell them."

Sewa laughed.

"Stories cannot remember people."

Grandma looked at her again.

"Are you sure?"

Sewa stopped laughing.

The night suddenly felt colder.

Grandma picked up a small piece of wood.

She drew a circle in the sand.

"Many years ago, beyond the red hills, there was a village called Oduka."

Sewa leaned forward.

"Was it a big village?"

Grandma nodded.

"Big enough for three markets, two streams, seven family compounds and one old baobab tree."

Sewa smiled.

"Seven compounds?"

"Seven."

"Why do I feel like the baobab tree is important?"

Grandma smiled.

"Because you are listening."

She stirred the fire.

The flames rose again.

"In Oduka lived a girl named Zuberi."

Sewa quickly interrupted.

"Zuberi was a boy's name."

Grandma laughed.

"Not in this story."

Sewa nodded.

Grandma continued.

Zuberi was fourteen years old.

She was quick with her feet, sharp with her eyes and always asking questions.

If an elder said, "Don't go there," Zuberi wanted to know why.

If someone said, "Don't touch that," she wanted to know what would happen if she touched it.

And if someone said, "Forget what you saw," Zuberi remembered it for years.

One afternoon, she was returning from the stream when she heard something behind the old baobab tree.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

She stopped.

The sound came again.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

Zuberi looked around.

Nobody was there.

She walked closer to the tree.

The ground was covered with dry leaves.

Nothing moved.

Then she heard a voice.

"Zuberi."

Sewa's eyes widened.

"Grandma!"

Grandma stopped.

"What?"

"The tree called her name?"

Grandma nodded.

"Yes."

Sewa looked toward the darkness behind Grandma.

"Did she answer?"

Grandma's face became serious.

"That is the mistake she made."

Sewa moved closer.

"What did she say?"

Grandma lowered her voice.

"Who is calling me?"

The fire cracked loudly.

Sewa jumped.

Grandma smiled.

"Don't be afraid."

"I'm not afraid."

"Good."

Grandma continued.

The moment Zuberi answered, the wind stopped.

Every leaf around the baobab tree became still.

Even the birds stopped singing.

Then something moved inside the tree.

Not outside.

Inside.

Zuberi placed her hand against the rough bark.

The tree was warm.

Too warm.

She pulled her hand away.

A small red mark was left on her palm.

Sewa interrupted again.

"What kind of mark?"

Grandma turned her palm upward.

She drew a small circle with her finger.

"A circle."

"Just a circle?"

"At first."

Sewa frowned.

"What happened later?"

Grandma looked into the fire.

"It opened."

Sewa became quiet.

Grandma continued.

That evening, Zuberi returned home.

She said nothing about the tree.

She washed her hands.

She ate her food.

She sat beside her mother.

She even laughed when her little brother spilled water on the floor.

But all through the evening, she kept looking at her palm.

The circle was still there.

Then something strange happened.

The circle moved.

It slowly turned like a tiny wheel beneath her skin.

Zuberi covered her hand.

Her mother noticed.

"What is wrong with your hand?"

Zuberi quickly shook her head.

"Nothing."

Her mother looked at her for a long moment.

Then she said something that frightened Zuberi.

"Did you go near the baobab?"

Zuberi froze.

Sewa leaned closer to Grandma.

"Her mother already knew?"

Grandma nodded.

"Some mothers know things their children think they have hidden."

"What did Zuberi say?"

Grandma stared at Sewa.

"She lied."

Sewa shook her head.

"Why?"

"Because fear makes people hide the truth."

Grandma picked up another piece of wood.

She placed it into the fire.

The flames grew.

"Zuberi said she had not gone near the tree."

Her mother said nothing.

She simply stood up and walked outside.

Zuberi watched her disappear into the darkness.

Then she heard her mother whisper something.

"Not again."

Zuberi followed her to the doorway.

Her mother was standing under the moon.

She was staring toward the old baobab.

Zuberi touched her shoulder.

"Mother?"

Her mother turned quickly.

"Go inside."

"Why?"

Her mother grabbed her wrist.

"Because it has found you."

Zuberi looked toward the tree.

The baobab stood far away.

Its branches were black against the moon.

Then one branch moved.

Slowly.

Like a hand.

Zuberi pulled away from her mother.

"What has found me?"

Her mother did not answer.

Instead, she pulled Zuberi inside and locked the door.

Then she pushed a heavy wooden bench against it.

Zuberi had never seen her mother so afraid.

"Mother, tell me."

Her mother looked at the girl's palm.

The red circle was glowing.

"Your grandmother had this mark."

Zuberi's eyes widened.

"Grandmother?"

Her mother nodded.

"Many years ago."

"What happened to her?"

Her mother became silent.

Zuberi waited.

Then came a sound from outside.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

Zuberi looked at the door.

Her mother closed her eyes.

"It has come."

Another sound followed.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

But this time, the sound was not coming from the door.

It was coming from the floor.

Zuberi looked down.

Something was tapping beneath the house.

Her mother grabbed her hand.

"Do not answer it."

The tapping stopped.

Silence filled the room.

Then a child's voice came from beneath the floor.

"Zuberi..."

Zuberi covered her mouth.

Her mother held her tightly.

The voice came again.

"Zuberi, I know you are there."

Sewa suddenly grabbed Grandma's arm.

"Grandma, who was under the house?"

Grandma did not answer.

Sewa waited.

Grandma slowly looked at her.

"That is where the story becomes dangerous."

Sewa swallowed.

"Tell me."

Grandma stared into the fire.

"The voice belonged to a child who had disappeared thirty years earlier."

Sewa's mouth opened.

"Was the child dead?"

Grandma shook her head.

"No."

She paused.

Then she whispered:

"That was the frightening part."

The fire suddenly went out.

Sewa screamed.

Grandma did not move.

From somewhere beyond the darkness came three soft taps.

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

Sewa looked at Grandma.

"Grandma..."

Grandma slowly reached for her hand.

"Don't answer."

A voice came from the darkness.

"Sewa..."

Grandma's face changed.

She knew that voice.

And before Sewa could ask who it was, Grandma whispered:

"That voice should have died with me."
