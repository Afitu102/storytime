🌙 Grandma's Story Beneath the Moon

🌍 African Folktales • 🕒 30 min read

🌙 Chapter One – Come Closer, My Children

"Come closer."

That was how Grandma Sewa always began her stories.

Not with a book.

Not with a drum.

Just three words.

"Come closer, my children."

So the children moved closer.

The young men moved closer too.

Even those who pretended not to care about old stories came to sit near her.

It was a cool night in the village.

The moon was bright.

It looked so close that little Timi stretched his hand toward it.

"Grandma, can someone touch the moon?"

Grandma Sewa laughed.

"If your hand is long enough."

Everyone laughed.

She shook her head and pulled her blue wrapper tighter around her shoulders.

Then she looked at the moon.

Her smile slowly left her face.

"But there was once a girl who came very close."

The laughter stopped.

Young Kelechi leaned forward.

"How close?"

Grandma Sewa looked at him.

"Close enough for the moon to know her name."

Nobody spoke.

Even the fire seemed to burn more quietly.

Grandma Sewa picked up a small stick.

She drew a circle in the soft earth.

"Her name was Amaka."

Amaka lived in a small village near a wide river.

She was not the richest girl in the village.

She did not wear fine beads.

She did not live in a big house.

But she had one thing that made people remember her.

She could hear things other people could not hear.

She could hear fish moving under the river.

She could hear birds sleeping inside trees.

She could hear the sound of rain long before clouds came.

But there was one sound she could never explain.

A soft voice that called her name whenever the moon was full.

"Amaka..."

The voice came from the forest.

Every full moon.

Always at midnight.

Amaka never told anyone.

Not even her mother.

She was afraid they would laugh.

One night, she decided to follow it.

She waited until everyone in the house was asleep.

She quietly opened the door.

The night air touched her face.

She looked back at the sleeping house.

Then she stepped outside.

The village was silent.

No dog barked.

No child cried.

Even the old palm trees were still.

Amaka walked toward the forest.

She carried a small lantern.

Its flame shook as she walked.

Then she heard it.

"Amaka..."

She stopped.

The voice was closer.

"Amaka..."

She raised the lantern.

Nothing.

Only trees.

Only leaves.

Only the bright moon above her.

She took another step.

A dry branch broke behind her.

Crack.

Amaka turned quickly.

Her heart jumped.

A small white goat stood on the path.

Amaka breathed out.

"You scared me."

The goat looked at her.

Then it walked away.

Amaka followed.

The goat stopped beside a large tree.

Amaka raised her lantern.

There was something carved into the tree.

A moon.

And beneath the moon was a small hand.

Amaka touched it.

The tree became warm.

She pulled her hand away.

The goat ran into the bushes.

Amaka followed it with her eyes.

Then she heard a sound.

Not from the forest.

From above.

She looked up.

The moon was moving.

Slowly.

Very slowly.

It was coming down.

Amaka stepped backward.

The light grew brighter.

The trees around her began to glow.

The river in the distance started shining like silver.

Then a woman appeared beneath the moon.

She wore a long white dress.

Her hair was silver.

Her feet did not touch the ground.

Amaka wanted to run.

But her legs would not move.

The woman looked at her.

"You came."

Amaka swallowed.

"Who are you?"

The woman smiled.

"Someone who has been waiting."

Amaka looked around.

"Waiting for me?"

The woman nodded.

"Yes."

Amaka wanted to ask why.

But before she could speak, the woman pointed toward the river.

"Look."

Amaka turned.

The river was moving backward.

Water was leaving the village.

The riverbed was slowly becoming dry.

Amaka's eyes widened.

"What is happening?"

The woman looked at her.

"The moon is tired."

Amaka shook her head.

"I don't understand."

The woman came closer.

Her voice became soft.

"Your village has forgotten something."

Amaka looked toward the village.

The lights of the houses were going out one by one.

A strange wind moved through the trees.

Then every bird in the forest flew into the sky at once.

Amaka looked up.

The moon was no longer round.

A dark mark had appeared across it.

The woman reached into her dress.

She brought out a small wooden key.

She placed it in Amaka's hand.

"Take this home."

Amaka looked at the key.

"What does it open?"

The woman smiled.

"You will know when you see the door."

Then the woman began to disappear.

Amaka reached for her.

"Wait!"

The woman looked back.

"And Amaka..."

Amaka froze.

"Do not let your grandmother hear the moon calling."

Then she vanished.

The forest became dark.

The river became silent.

Amaka stood alone.

She looked at the wooden key.

A tiny word was carved into it.

HOME.

Amaka ran.

She ran through the trees.

She ran past the old palm tree.

She ran across the narrow path.

She ran until she reached the first house in the village.

Then she stopped.

Her mother was standing outside.

Her father was beside her.

And behind them stood Grandma Sewa.

Grandma Sewa was not smiling.

She was staring at the key in Amaka's hand.

Amaka slowly walked toward them.

"Grandma..."

The old woman raised one hand.

"Where did you get that?"

Amaka looked at the key.

"The moon gave it to me."

Grandma Sewa's face changed.

She looked toward the sky.

The moon was still there.

But now it was almost completely dark.

Grandma Sewa whispered something.

Amaka could not hear it.

Then the old woman turned to everyone.

"Nobody leaves this house tonight."

The wind suddenly stopped.

A knock came from the forest.

Three slow knocks.

Knock.

Knock.

Knock.

Grandma Sewa looked at Amaka.

For the first time, the old woman looked afraid.

"She has found us."

Amaka held the wooden key tighter.

Then something knocked again.

But this time...

the sound came from inside the house.

🌙 End of Chapter One

🌿 Chapter Three – Promise Beneath the Moon

Grandma Sewa did not sleep that night.

She sat beside the dead fire.

Her hands rested on her walking stick.

Amaka sat close to her mother.

Her father stood near the window.

Nobody wanted to speak first.

Outside, the village was quiet.

Too quiet.

Then Grandma Sewa finally spoke.

"Some stories are better told before the sun comes up."

Amaka looked at her.

"Then tell us."

Grandma Sewa looked toward the window.

The moon was still hiding behind dark clouds.

"Many years ago, this village was almost destroyed by a long dry season."

The river became small.

The farms became dry.

The goats had little grass to eat.

People walked far just to find water.

Children woke up thirsty.

Even the old trees began to lose their leaves.

One night, the elders gathered beside the river.

They asked for rain.

They prayed.

They sang.

They waited.

But nothing happened.

Then a young woman came to the river.

Her name was Nneka.

She carried a small wooden bowl.

She stood at the riverbank and looked at the moon.

The moon was bright that night.

Nneka raised the bowl.

"If you can hear us, please help my people."

The river moved.

Just once.

A small wave touched her feet.

Nneka stepped backward.

Then a voice came from the water.

"What will you give?"

Nneka looked around.

Nobody was there.

"I will give anything."

The voice answered.

"Anything?"

Nneka nodded.

"Anything."

The moon became brighter.

Rain began to fall.

At first, it was only a few drops.

Then it became heavy.

The dry river filled.

The people shouted with joy.

Children danced in the rain.

Farmers ran into their fields.

Nobody asked what Nneka had promised.

But Nneka knew.

She had promised that whenever the moon called, someone from her family would answer.

Grandma Sewa stopped speaking.

Amaka stared at her.

"That was the promise?"

Grandma Sewa nodded.

"Yes."

Amaka looked at the moon.

"But why is it calling me?"

Grandma Sewa lowered her eyes.

"Because the promise was never finished."

Amaka's father stepped forward.

"Mother, enough."

Grandma Sewa looked at him.

"You know I am right."

He looked away.

Amaka stood.

"What are you hiding from me?"

Her father said nothing.

Amaka looked at her mother.

Her mother was crying quietly.

"Mum?"

Her mother wiped her face.

"I remember the river now."

Amaka moved closer.

"What happened there?"

Her mother took a slow breath.

"I was there when your grandmother tried to end the promise."

Grandma Sewa tightened her grip on the walking stick.

"We made a mistake."

Amaka waited.

Her mother continued.

"We thought we could hide the promise."

The wind moved through the room.

"So we hid the key."

Amaka looked down.

The wooden key was still in her hand.

Her face became pale.

"You hid it?"

Grandma Sewa nodded.

"We hid it inside this house."

Amaka looked around.

The old walls.

The wooden cupboard.

The small windows.

The fire.

Everything suddenly felt different.

Then three knocks came from beneath the floor.

Knock.

Knock.

Knock.

Everyone froze.

Amaka looked down.

"Something is under us."

Grandma Sewa stood slowly.

She walked to the middle of the room.

She tapped the floor with her stick.

One part sounded empty.

She tapped again.

The same sound.

Amaka's father moved closer.

Together, they pushed aside an old mat.

There was a small wooden door beneath it.

Amaka stared at it.

The key in her hand became warm again.

"That is the door."

Grandma Sewa nodded.

"Yes."

Amaka placed the key into the lock.

It turned by itself.

Click.

The door opened.

A cold wind came from below.

There was a narrow staircase.

It went deep under the house.

Amaka lifted the lantern.

The light showed old drawings on the walls.

A moon.

A river.

A woman.

And a small child.

Amaka moved closer.

The child in the drawing looked like her.

She touched the wall.

The drawing changed.

The child became older.

Then older again.

Then it became Amaka.

She pulled her hand away.

"Grandma..."

The old woman stepped beside her.

"Don't be afraid."

Amaka looked at the dark stairs.

"Where does this lead?"

Grandma Sewa looked down.

"To the place where Nneka made her promise."

A soft sound came from below.

Someone was humming.

The same song from the forest.

Amaka held the lantern tighter.

Then a child's voice came from the darkness.

"Amaka..."

She stopped.

"Who is there?"

The voice laughed softly.

"You came back."

Amaka looked at Grandma Sewa.

The old woman had gone completely still.

"Grandma?"

Grandma Sewa whispered one name.

"Nneka."

The lantern flame suddenly went out.

Darkness swallowed the staircase.

Then two small feet appeared on the step below Amaka.

Bare feet.

Wet with river water.

Amaka raised the lantern.

The flame came back.

A little girl stood in the darkness.

She looked about eight years old.

She wore a blue cloth.

The same blue cloth Grandma Sewa had found.

The girl looked at Amaka.

Then she smiled.

"Grandma told you the wrong story."

Amaka felt the cold run through her body.

"What do you mean?"

The girl pointed deeper into the darkness.

"Ask her who really made the promise."

Amaka turned toward Grandma Sewa.

The old woman was crying.

And for the first time that night, she had no answer.

🌙 End of Chapter Three

🌿 Chapter Four – What Grandma Never Told

Nobody moved.

The little girl stood on the dark stairs.

Water dripped from her blue cloth.

Drop.

Drop.

Drop.

Amaka looked at Grandma Sewa.

"Who really made the promise?"

Grandma Sewa looked down.

She did not answer.

The little girl smiled.

"She knows."

Amaka stepped closer to her grandmother.

"Tell me."

Grandma Sewa held her walking stick tightly.

"I was afraid you would ask one day."

"Then tell me now."

The old woman took a slow breath.

"Nneka did not make the promise."

Amaka frowned.

"Then who did?"

Grandma Sewa looked at the little girl on the stairs.

The girl was gone.

Only wet footprints remained.

Grandma Sewa whispered.

"Her mother."

Amaka's mother stepped forward.

"Grandma..."

The old woman raised her hand.

"Let me finish."

She looked at Amaka.

"Many years ago, my mother stood beside this same river."

Amaka listened.

"She was young. She had three children. One of them was Nneka."

Grandma Sewa slowly walked down the stairs.

Amaka followed.

Her father came behind them.

Her mother stayed close.

The staircase seemed longer than before.

The walls were wet.

Small drops of water moved across the stones.

But the water was moving upward.

Amaka watched one drop climb the wall.

She blinked.

It was gone.

Grandma Sewa kept walking.

"My mother believed the moon watched over the village."

"She believed it could bring rain."

"And one year, when the village was dying from hunger, she went to ask for help."

They reached the bottom.

A large stone room opened before them.

There was no roof.

Moonlight came through a round hole above.

In the middle of the room was a dry well.

Amaka walked toward it.

She heard water below.

Her father grabbed her arm.

"Don't lean over."

Amaka stopped.

Grandma Sewa stood beside the well.

"This is where it happened."

Amaka looked inside.

There was no water.

Only darkness.

Then a child's laugh came from below.

Amaka stepped back.

"That sound..."

Her mother nodded.

"I heard it too."

The old woman continued.

"My mother asked the moon for rain."

"The moon answered."

Amaka waited.

"What did it want?"

Grandma Sewa looked at the well.

"A promise."

Thunder sounded far away.

Grandma Sewa spoke slowly.

"She promised that one child from every generation would come to the river when the moon called."

"But she never said what would happen to that child."

Amaka looked at her.

"What happens?"

Grandma Sewa's eyes filled with tears.

"Nobody knows."

A loud splash came from the well.

Everyone jumped.

Water suddenly rose from the darkness.

It reached the edge.

Then stopped.

A small wooden bowl floated on top.

Amaka recognized it.

It was the bowl from Grandma Sewa's story.

She reached for it.

Her father stopped her.

"Wait."

The bowl moved by itself.

It floated toward Amaka.

Then it stopped at her feet.

Inside it was a small piece of cloth.

Blue.

Amaka picked it up.

The moment her fingers touched it, the room changed.

She was no longer underground.

She stood beside the river.

The sky was dark.

A young woman stood near the water.

She was crying.

A little girl held her hand.

The little girl was Nneka.

Amaka knew it at once.

Another woman stood behind them.

She was holding the wooden bowl.

The moon shone above them.

Then a voice came from the river.

"Choose."

The young woman shook her head.

"No."

The voice came again.

"One child."

The young woman hugged Nneka.

"Take me instead."

The river became still.

Then the moonlight moved toward her.

The young woman disappeared.

Nneka screamed.

Amaka tried to run toward her.

But she could not move.

Then someone touched her shoulder.

Amaka turned.

Grandma Sewa was standing behind her.

"Come back."

Amaka opened her eyes.

She was back in the underground room.

Her mother was holding her.

Her father stood near the well.

Grandma Sewa looked frightened.

"What did you see?"

Amaka looked at her.

"Nneka's mother."

Grandma Sewa nodded.

"What happened to her?"

Amaka looked toward the well.

"She gave herself instead of Nneka."

Nobody spoke.

Then Amaka noticed something.

A small white flower had appeared on the edge of the well.

She touched it.

A voice whispered from inside.

"She never left."

Amaka pulled her hand away.

The water inside the well began to rise.

Higher.

Higher.

Then a hand came out.

A woman's hand.

Grandma Sewa stepped backward.

"No..."

The hand rested on the edge of the well.

Then another hand appeared.

Slowly, a woman climbed out.

Her dress was wet.

Her hair covered her face.

She stood quietly before them.

Amaka could barely breathe.

The woman lifted her head.

Her eyes were calm.

"Enough."

Grandma Sewa dropped her walking stick.

"Mother..."

The woman looked at her.

"You let them believe the wrong story."

Amaka looked at Grandma Sewa.

"Is that your mother?"

Grandma Sewa nodded.

The woman turned to Amaka.

"Your family was never meant to pay the price."

Amaka stepped forward.

"Then why is the moon calling me?"

The woman smiled.

"Because the promise has changed."

The moonlight above them suddenly became bright.

A long crack appeared across the ceiling.

Wind rushed through the room.

The woman pointed toward Amaka.

"The moon is no longer asking for a child."

Amaka waited.

"What does it want?"

The woman looked toward the dark tunnel behind them.

"It wants someone to open the gate."

A deep sound came from the tunnel.

Something heavy moved in the darkness.

Grandma Sewa picked up her walking stick.

"Nobody goes there."

The woman looked at her.

"That is where you left the truth."

Then the tunnel lights came on.

One after another.

At the end stood a large wooden gate.

It had no handle.

No lock.

Only one small hole.

The same shape as Amaka's wooden key.

Amaka looked at the key in her hand.

Then she looked at Grandma Sewa.

The old woman whispered:

"If you open that gate, there will be no going back."

Amaka took one step toward it.

Then another.

Behind the gate, something began to sing.

It was a woman's voice.

And Amaka knew the song.

It was the lullaby her mother had sung to her since she was a baby.

Amaka stopped in front of the gate.

She raised the key.

Then the voice behind the gate whispered her name.

"Amaka..."

The key turned in her hand.

🌙 End of Chapter Four

🌕 Chapter Five – Door Beneath the Earth

The key turned.

Click.

Amaka held her breath.

Nobody spoke.

The old wooden gate moved by itself.

It opened slowly.

Creeeak.

A warm wind came from the other side.

It carried the smell of fresh grass.

Then came the sound of birds.

Amaka looked at Grandma Sewa.

"Is there another forest behind this gate?"

Grandma Sewa shook her head.

"There should not be."

Amaka stepped closer.

Beyond the gate was a wide green field.

The moon hung low in the sky.

Small white flowers covered the ground.

A narrow path crossed the field.

At the end stood a small house.

Smoke came from its roof.

Amaka stared.

"Who lives there?"

The woman who had come from the well looked at her.

"Someone who has been waiting for you."

Amaka stepped through the gate.

Her mother grabbed her hand.

"Don't go alone."

Amaka nodded.

They walked together.

Grandma Sewa followed behind them.

Her father carried the lantern.

The field was quiet.

But it did not feel empty.

The flowers moved as they passed.

Some bent toward Amaka.

Others turned toward the moon.

A small white bird flew over their heads.

It landed on a tree near the house.

Then it began to sing.

The song was soft.

Almost like a lullaby.

Amaka stopped.

Her mother stopped too.

"That song..."

Her mother covered her mouth.

"I know it."

Grandma Sewa looked at her.

"Everyone in our family knows it."

Amaka listened.

The little bird finished the song.

Then it flew toward the house.

The front door opened.

Nobody touched it.

A little boy stood inside.

He looked about ten years old.

He wore a simple brown shirt.

In his hand was a wooden spoon.

He smiled at Amaka.

"Grandma said you would come."

Amaka looked behind her.

Grandma Sewa was not there.

Her mother was not there.

Her father was not there.

Amaka was standing alone.

The gate had disappeared.

She turned around.

Only the green field remained.

The boy laughed softly.

"Don't worry."

Amaka looked at him.

"Where is my family?"

"They are still on the other side."

"Then why can't I see them?"

The boy pointed at the moon.

"Because this place only shows what people are ready to remember."

Amaka did not understand.

The boy stepped aside.

"Come inside."

Amaka entered.

The house was small.

There was a wooden table.

Two chairs.

A clay pot.

And a small fire burning near the wall.

A picture hung above the fire.

Amaka walked toward it.

Her heart stopped.

It showed her mother as a young girl.

Beside her stood Grandma Sewa.

And beside them stood Nneka.

But there was another person in the picture.

A young man.

Amaka did not know him.

She pointed at the picture.

"Who is that?"

The boy looked at it.

"Your grandfather."

Amaka stared.

"I never knew him."

"Most people in your family don't."

The boy walked toward the table.

He placed the wooden spoon down.

"He was the first person who tried to break the promise."

Amaka turned.

"Did he fail?"

The boy nodded.

"He opened this door."

Amaka looked around.

"This place?"

The boy nodded again.

"This place."

Amaka walked toward the window.

The green field stretched far into the distance.

She could see the moon above it.

But something was wrong.

There was no sky behind the moon.

Only darkness.

Amaka stepped back.

"What is this place?"

The boy looked at her.

"A place between yesterday and tomorrow."

Amaka shook her head.

"That makes no sense."

The boy smiled.

"Grandma says the same thing."

Amaka laughed for the first time that night.

The boy smiled too.

Then a loud bang came from outside.

"Long ago, a family made a promise."

The villagers listened.

Amaka told them about Adesewa.

She told them about the river.

She told them about the forgotten names.

She told them about the wooden key.

She told them about the bells.

She told them about the night the family became afraid.

Nobody interrupted her.

Even the wind seemed to wait.

When Amaka finished, Adesewa stepped forward.

She placed the blue cloth in Amaka's hand.

"Keep this."

Amaka looked at it.

"Why?"

Adesewa smiled.

"Because every family needs something that reminds them where they came from."

Then she turned toward the river.

One by one, the forgotten villagers followed her.

Their lanterns became brighter.

Then their bodies began to fade.

Amaka stepped forward.

"Are you leaving?"

Adesewa looked back.

"No."

"We are going where every remembered story belongs."

The villagers disappeared into the moonlight.

Only their lanterns remained.

Then the lanterns turned into tiny stars.

They rose into the sky.

Amaka watched until the last one disappeared.

Grandpa placed a hand on her shoulder.

"Now you can go home."

Amaka looked at the village.

For the first time, she felt no fear.

She felt peace.

But before they left, she heard something.

A bell.

One soft ring.

She turned.

The small wooden house was empty.

But on its table was another letter.

Amaka walked toward it.

She picked it up.

Her name was written on the front.

She opened it.

There were only three words.

"Keep telling stories."

Amaka smiled.

Then she folded the letter.

She placed it safely inside the wooden box.

The old tree moved gently in the wind.

The river made a soft sound.

And somewhere far away, a child laughed.

Amaka looked at Grandma Sewa.

"Let's go home."

Grandma Sewa nodded.

They walked together.

But just before Amaka crossed the old wooden gate, she looked back.

For one brief moment, she saw Adesewa standing beneath the tree.

She smiled.

Then she was gone.

🌙 End of Chapter Nine

🌅 Chapter Ten – Grandma's Story Beneath the Moon

The morning came quietly.

No bell rang.

No strange voice came from the river.

No white bird appeared in the trees.

Only the sound of birds filled the village.

Amaka opened her eyes.

She was lying beneath the old tree.

For a few seconds, she did not move.

She looked at the sky.

The moon was still there.

It was fading slowly as the sun came up.

Amaka touched the blue cloth in her hand.

It was still there.

She smiled.

So it had not been a dream.

She sat up.

Grandma Sewa was sitting beside the fire.

She was roasting corn.

Her old wooden stool stood beside her.

She looked at Amaka and smiled.

"You are awake."

Amaka stood and walked toward her.

"Grandma."

Grandma Sewa handed her a roasted corn.

"Eat first."

Amaka laughed.

"After everything that happened, you want me to eat?"

Grandma Sewa laughed too.

"That is exactly why you should eat."

Amaka sat beside her.

Her mother and father were nearby.

They were speaking with the villagers.

Everything looked normal.

But Amaka knew things had changed.

People were talking about the names that had been forgotten.

Old family stories were being told again.

Children sat around the elders.

They listened.

Nobody was afraid anymore.

Amaka looked at Grandma Sewa.

"Will people believe what happened?"

Grandma Sewa smiled.

"They do not have to believe everything."

"They only need to remember."

Amaka nodded.

She reached into the wooden box.

The letters were still inside.

The little clay whistle was there.

The red thread was there.

And the old photograph.

She looked at it.

Adesewa was standing beside Nneka.

Grandma Sewa was there too.

Amaka's grandfather stood behind them.

Everyone was smiling.

Amaka touched Adesewa's face on the picture.

"She wanted to be remembered."

Grandma Sewa nodded.

"Sometimes that is all a person wants."

Amaka looked at the children gathering under the tree.

One little girl came running toward them.

"Grandma Sewa!"

Grandma Sewa looked at her.

"What is it?"

The girl pointed at Amaka.

"She knows the story, right?"

Grandma Sewa looked at Amaka.

Amaka smiled.

"Yes."

The little girl sat down.

Soon more children came.

Then young men.

Then young women.

They all sat beneath the tree.

Grandma Sewa looked around.

"Well?"

Amaka laughed.

"What?"

"They are waiting."

Amaka looked at the group.

She understood.

They wanted the story.

She sat beneath the tree.

The children moved closer.

The morning breeze passed through the branches.

Grandma Sewa placed the roasted corn beside her.

Amaka held the blue cloth.

She looked at the faces around her.

Then she began.

"Long ago, before any of you were born, there was a family that lived beside a river."

The children became quiet.

Amaka continued.

"They had a secret."

One boy leaned forward.

"What secret?"

Amaka smiled.

"That is where the story begins."

Grandma Sewa laughed softly.

Amaka told them about the old river.

She told them about the white bird.

She told them about the strange door.

She told them about the forgotten people.

She told them about Adesewa.

She told them about Grandpa.

She told them about the bells.

She told them about the promise.

The children listened without making a sound.

Even the adults stopped their work.

They listened too.

When Amaka reached the part about the forgotten names, she paused.

"Do you know what I learned?"

The little girl shook her head.

"What?"

Amaka smiled.

"A person can disappear from a house."

"A person can disappear from a photograph."

"A person can even disappear from a book."

She looked at Grandma Sewa.

"But a good story can bring them home."

The children smiled.

Grandma Sewa's eyes became wet.

Amaka continued the story.

The sun climbed higher.

The fire became smaller.

The village became bright.

And still they listened.

When Amaka finally reached the end, she closed the wooden box.

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Then the little girl asked:

"Is Adesewa still there?"

Amaka looked toward the old tree.

A white bird was sitting on one of the branches.

Amaka smiled.

"Maybe."

The bird flew into the sky.

The children watched it.

Then the little girl smiled.

"I think she is listening."

Amaka smiled.

"I think so too."

Grandma Sewa stood slowly.

She looked at the children.

"Never laugh at an old story."

"You may not know whose voice is hiding inside it."

Everyone became quiet.

Grandma Sewa looked toward the river.

A soft breeze moved across the water.

Then a single bell rang.

Amaka looked up.

Grandma Sewa smiled.

"That is enough for today."

The children protested.

"Grandma, tell us more!"

Grandma Sewa laughed.

"Another night."

The children ran away.

Amaka stayed beneath the tree.

She looked at the blue cloth.

Then she folded it carefully.

She placed it inside the wooden box.

The wind moved again.

A small white flower landed beside her.

Amaka picked it up.

She smiled.

She knew the story was finished.

But she also knew something else.

Stories never really end.

They move from one person to another.

From grandmother to mother.

From mother to child.

From child to another child.

And as long as someone remembers, the people inside the story are never truly gone.

🌿 Moral of the Story

Never be ashamed of where you come from.

Do not erase the people who came before you.

Listen to old stories because they can carry lessons that books may never teach.

Most of all, do not let fear make you hide the truth from the people you love.

A family becomes stronger when it remembers its past, learns from it, and carries the good parts into the future.

And sometimes, the story you almost forgot may be the story someone else needs to hear.

📖 The End
