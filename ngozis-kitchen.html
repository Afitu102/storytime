🍲 Ngozi's Kitchen

🌍 African Folktales • 🕒 30 min read

💔 Chapter One – The Money That Disappeared

Ngozi stood in the kitchen, staring at the plates she had washed only a few minutes earlier. The house was quiet, but the silence between her and her husband, Chidi, felt heavier than any noise.

For months, something had been wrong with their money. They worked hard through their catering business, yet there was always another shortage.

That evening, Chidi came into the kitchen holding his phone.

“Ngozi, how many times must I ask where Saturday's money went?”

Ngozi turned around slowly.

“I gave you every kobo from that event myself, Chidi.”

“Then explain why the account is short again this month.”

“I cook for every client, Chidi. I don't touch the money.”

Chidi stared at her.

“Someone is touching it, and it isn't disappearing on its own.”

Ngozi wiped her hands with a cloth and stepped closer.

“Then show me the ledger.”

“Why?”

“Because I want to see where the money went. Let us look together, Chidi. Some expenses are mine to manage, not yours to question.”

His face hardened.

“Every expense in this house is mine to question, Ngozi.”

Ngozi said nothing.

She had spent years building their catering business with him. While Chidi dealt with customers and payments, she was the one standing over hot pots, preparing meals, tasting sauces, arranging trays and making sure every event went well.

But lately, Chidi had become secretive.

He changed passwords. He took calls outside. And whenever she asked about the missing money, he became angry.

That night, after Chidi went to bed, Ngozi remained awake.

Something inside her refused to let the matter go.

She picked up her phone and checked the account records.

At first, everything looked normal.

Then she saw one name.

Foonmi Adeyemi.

Ngozi frowned.

She opened the transaction.

Her eyes moved slowly across the screen.

Transfer successful.

Foonmi Adeyemi.

Monthly rent payment.

Ngozi's heart began to beat faster.

She checked the date.

Then another payment appeared.

And another.

She kept scrolling.

Month after month.

Year after year.

Six years.

Ngozi covered her mouth with her hand.

“Six years?” she whispered.

Her husband had been paying another woman's rent for six years.

All those years, Ngozi had believed they were struggling because business was sometimes slow. She had cut down on her own needs. She had delayed buying new clothes. She had even borrowed money once to pay for ingredients for an important catering job.

Meanwhile, Chidi had been sending money somewhere else.

Ngozi looked toward the bedroom.

Chidi was still asleep.

She wanted to wake him immediately.

She wanted to scream.

She wanted answers.

But instead, she placed the phone on the table.

“I will not scream. I will not break tonight,” she whispered.

She walked into the small room where she kept her mother's things.

Her mother had died years earlier, but Ngozi still kept one special possession: an old handwritten recipe notebook.

She opened it carefully.

Inside were recipes her mother had written by hand, along with little notes about how to prepare each meal properly.

Ngozi touched the pages gently.

“Mama,” she whispered, “I don't know what is happening to my life.”

She closed the notebook and held it against her chest.

The next morning, she confronted Chidi.

“Who is Foonmi, Chidi?”

He stopped moving.

“What are you talking about?”

“Who is Foonmi Chidi? And why are you paying Foonmi's rent?”

Chidi looked away.

“Did you go through my phone?”

“The messages appeared. I saw the transfers too.”

He sighed.

“It is not what you think, Ngozi. It is complicated.”

Ngozi's eyes filled with tears.

“Complicated enough to starve this house every single month.”

Chidi remained silent.

“How long, Chidi?”

He looked at her.

“Does it matter now? You already have your answer.”

“It matters to me. Say it plainly, for once.”

Chidi's voice became cold.

“Since you no longer trust me, pack your things before morning.”

Ngozi stared at him.

She could not believe what she had just heard.

“You are choosing her over this marriage. Say it, Chidi.”

He said nothing.

That silence was enough.

That night, Ngozi knelt beside her bed.

“Father, he chose another woman over the house I built.”

She wiped her tears.

“Do not let his lies decide what I believe about myself.”

Her voice shook.

“Whatever is truly mine, Lord, let it come with me.”

She looked toward the old recipe notebook.

“Give me strength to walk out with my head high.”

She held the notebook tightly.

“Mama, help me carry what you started.”

Before sunrise, Ngozi packed a small bag.

She took a few clothes, her mother's recipe notebook, and the things she could carry without looking back.

She left the recipe cards behind.

Those cards belonged to the business.

But her mother's notebook belonged to her.

When morning came, Chidi stood by the door.

“I meant what I said last night. Take your bag and go.”

Ngozi looked at him.

“Space to think or space for Foonmi to move in?”

“That is not your concern anymore. Just go, Ngozi.”

She picked up her bag.

Before leaving, she looked around the house one last time.

“Every dish that built this business came from my hands, Chidi.”

Chidi answered without hesitation.

“Clients came for my name, Ngozi, not your cooking.”

Ngozi looked straight into his eyes.

“Then let us see how far your name carries you.”

She walked out.

Chidi watched her disappear down the road.

He did not know that the woman walking away with one small bag was carrying something far more valuable than money.

She was carrying the knowledge, skill and recipes that had made his business successful.

And she was about to discover what those hands could build without him.

🌙 End of Chapter One

🚪 Chapter Two – The Woman Who Opened Her Door

Chidi waited until Ngozi was gone before picking up his phone.

He stared at the screen for a moment, then called Foonmi.

“It is done, Foonmi. I have chased her away. You do not have to hide anymore. You can stay.”

There was silence on the other end before Foonmi answered.

“Oh, Chidi, thank you. Finally, Chidi, this house is truly ours now.”

Chidi sat down heavily.

“But her cooking built this business, Foonmi.”

Foonmi laughed softly.

“She is not important. We only need her recipe cards.”

“You think it will be that simple?”

“Relax, Chidi. Anyone can follow written ingredients.”

Chidi looked toward the kitchen.

For years, Ngozi had been the one making sure every meal tasted right. But he pushed the thought away.

He believed the recipe cards were enough.

He did not know how much he was about to lose.

Meanwhile, Ngozi walked along the road with her bag hanging from her shoulder.

She did not know where she would sleep that night.

She did not know what she would eat.

She only knew that she could not go back.

Her eyes were still wet, but she refused to cry in front of strangers.

She held her mother's old notebook tightly against her chest.

Every few steps, she looked down at it.

“Mama, I don't know where I am going,” she whispered.

“But I will keep going.”

She walked until her legs became tired.

Then she noticed a small food stall by the roadside.

There were several large pots sitting over gas burners. The smell of pepper, onions and fresh soup filled the air.

Ngozi stopped.

Her stomach tightened.

She had not eaten since the previous night.

A woman stood beside one of the pots, stirring carefully.

She was older than Ngozi, but her movements were quick and confident.

Ngozi sat quietly on a bench nearby.

She did not ask for food.

She only wanted to rest her feet.

Suddenly, she noticed something.

The soup in one of the pots was beginning to catch at the bottom.

Ngozi leaned forward.

“Madam, your soup is about to catch at the bottom.”

The woman immediately stopped stirring.

She looked at Ngozi.

“How did you smell that from all the way over there?”

Ngozi stood up.

“Don't scrape the bottom. Pour it into a clean pot now.”

The woman stared at her for a moment.

Then she quickly followed the instruction.

She poured the soup into another clean pot and tasted it.

“One scrape and the burnt taste ruins everything, Ngozi.”

Ngozi smiled faintly.

“It only needs one small pinch more.”

The woman's eyes narrowed.

“Bold words from a woman still sitting on my bench.”

Ngozi looked down.

“I'm sorry, madam. I did not mean to interfere.”

The woman tasted the soup again.

Her expression changed.

She added a small pinch of seasoning.

Then she tasted it once more.

“Whoever taught you this, they taught you well. Your hands know this pot well.”

Ngozi looked at the woman.

“Where did you learn?”

“My mother, madam. I have cooked since I was young.”

The woman studied her carefully.

Then she looked at the bag beside Ngozi.

“Why is a woman with hands like this carrying a packed bag?”

Ngozi lowered her eyes.

“My husband put me out this morning, madam.”

The woman became quiet.

“Did he give you somewhere safe to go, at least?”

“He gave me nothing, madam. Not even an explanation that made sense.”

The woman looked at her for a few seconds.

Then she asked gently,

“Have you eaten today?”

Ngozi shook her head.

“Not since last night.”

The woman lifted the lid of another pot.

“Then come, food first. Everything else can wait.”

Ngozi hesitated.

“I can't ask a stranger to feed me.”

The woman shook her head.

“You didn't ask, Ngozi. I offered it myself.”

Ngozi's eyes filled with tears.

She had spent the entire morning feeling like she had been thrown away.

Yet a complete stranger had noticed that she was hungry.

She sat down and ate quietly.

The woman watched her without asking too many questions.

After the meal, Ngozi opened her bag to take out her mother's notebook.

The woman noticed it immediately.

“Whose handwriting is this? It is filled with such care.”

Ngozi touched the cover gently.

“My mother's, madam. She wrote every recipe before she died.”

The woman nodded slowly.

“Your mother must have been a good woman.”

“She was, madam. She taught me everything I know.”

The woman looked at Ngozi again.

Something about her story seemed to touch a place in her heart.

“Come and stay with me tonight, Ngozi.”

Ngozi looked up quickly.

“Stay with you?”

“Yes.”

“I don't want to be a burden.”

The woman smiled.

“I know enough. Hungry, hurting, and too proud to ask.”

Ngozi remained silent.

“The room is small, but it is clean and warm.”

Ngozi swallowed hard.

“Why are you doing this for me?”

The woman looked away for a moment.

“Someone once did the same for me many years ago.”

Ngozi waited for her to continue.

But the woman only smiled.

“That is a story for another night, Ngozi.”

That evening, Ngozi followed the woman home.

It was a modest place, but it was peaceful.

For the first time since leaving Chidi's house, Ngozi felt safe.

The woman prepared a small room for her.

“You can sleep here.”

Ngozi placed her bag down.

“Thank you, madam.”

“My name is Adeze.”

Ngozi smiled.

“Thank you, Madam Adeze.”

That night, Ngozi lay awake thinking about everything that had happened.

Chidi's words still hurt.

“Clients came for my name, Ngozi, not your cooking.”

She closed her eyes.

She remembered all the meals she had prepared.

All the customers who had praised her food.

All the events where people had asked who cooked the meals.

For years, she had allowed herself to believe that her work was only part of Chidi's business.

But Madam Adeze had seen something different in her hands within minutes.

The next morning, Madam Adeze found Ngozi already awake.

“You are up early.”

Ngozi smiled.

“I have been waiting for a first morning, madam.”

Madam Adeze looked at her carefully.

“Then let today be that morning.”

Ngozi looked surprised.

“What do you mean?”

“Come to my store.”

“I don't want to take advantage of your kindness.”

Madam Adeze shook her head.

“You will not be taking advantage of anything.”

She picked up her keys.

“One week, Ngozi. Show me what those hands can do.”

Ngozi smiled.

“I will not disappoint you.”

Madam Adeze opened the door.

“Whatever pain you carry, leave it outside the store.”

Ngozi looked back at the small room she had slept in.

Then she picked up her mother's notebook.

She followed Madam Adeze outside.

Neither woman knew it yet, but that simple walk to the food stall was the beginning of something that would change Ngozi's life forever.

🌙 End of Chapter Two

🍲 Chapter Three – The Hands Behind the Food

The next morning, Ngozi arrived at Madam Adeze's food stall before most of the customers.

She tied her apron, washed her hands and looked around the small kitchen.

Madam Adeze watched her quietly.

“Whatever pain you carry, leave it outside the store,” she had told her the day before.

Ngozi had taken those words seriously.

She was not there to think about Chidi.

She was there to work.

“What should I start with, madam?” Ngozi asked.

Madam Adeze smiled.

“Start with what you know.”

Ngozi opened the pots and checked the ingredients.

Within minutes, she was moving around the kitchen as if she had worked there for years.

She checked the heat.

She tasted the soup.

She adjusted the seasoning.

She watched the meat carefully.

Madam Adeze stood at the side, watching every movement.

“You don't measure everything?” she asked.

Ngozi smiled.

“Some things are better measured with experience.”

Madam Adeze nodded.

“I knew there was something different about you.”

By afternoon, the stall became busy.

Customers kept coming.

One customer took the first bite of her food and looked up immediately.

“Madam, who cooked this?”

Madam Adeze pointed toward Ngozi.

“She did.”

The customer smiled.

“Tell her not to stop. This is very good.”

Another customer ordered the same meal.

Then another.

By evening, Ngozi had helped prepare forty catering trays.

Madam Adeze looked at the empty containers in disbelief.

“We have never finished this many trays in one day.”

Ngozi wiped her hands.

“I have cooked for bigger events than this, madam. Trust me.”

Madam Adeze laughed.

“I am beginning to.”

The following days became even busier.

Customers who had tasted Ngozi's food returned.

Some came with friends.

Others asked for the same meals they had eaten before.

By the end of the first week, Madam Adeze called Ngozi aside.

“Customers returned every day because of your food.”

Ngozi looked down modestly.

“I only did what I know.”

Madam Adeze shook her head.

“No. You did more than that.”

She handed Ngozi the keys to the storage room.

“The trial is over. This stall is yours to help run now.”

Ngozi's eyes widened.

“Madam, are you sure?”

“I am sure.”

Ngozi hugged her.

“Thank you.”

Madam Adeze smiled.

“When your own door opens, hold it for another woman.”

Ngozi nodded.

“One day, madam, I will do exactly that.”

But while Ngozi's life was beginning to change, things were going in the opposite direction at Chidi's catering business.

At first, Chidi was confident.

He had the recipe cards Ngozi had left behind.

He believed that was all he needed.

Foonmi stood in the kitchen with the cards spread across the table.

“This one says two cups of this and three spoons of that,” she said.

“Exactly,” Chidi replied.

“Then why are you worried?”

Chidi looked at the food.

“Because it does not taste the same.”

Foonmi frowned.

“We followed the recipe exactly.”

Chidi tasted the food again.

He shook his head.

“Ngozi made it taste different.”

Foonmi folded her arms.

“She only followed these cards.”

Chidi looked at her.

“It is not the card, Foonmi. It is knowing when to stop.”

Foonmi became irritated.

“So what are you saying? That we cannot cook without her?”

Chidi did not answer.

The first cancellation came a few days later.

Then another.

Then a third.

“Another cancellation?” Foonmi asked.

Chidi dropped his phone on the table.

“That is the third client this month.”

“Maybe they found a cheaper caterer.”

“Three clients?” Chidi asked. “All at once?”

Foonmi said nothing.

Chidi's biggest problem was that customers were beginning to notice the difference.

The food looked almost the same.

The ingredients were almost the same.

But something was missing.

The taste that had made people come back was gone.

One afternoon, Chidi received an important order.

He needed a large catering job completed perfectly.

He turned to Foonmi.

“I need you to handle this.”

Foonmi smiled.

“Leave it to me.”

The kitchen became busy.

Assistants moved around with trays and cooking pots.

Among them was Tolu, one of the workers who had been with Chidi for some time.

As Foonmi rushed around the kitchen, Tolu watched her carefully.

“Madam, are you sure this is ready?”

Foonmi looked at her.

“Why wouldn't it be?”

“Something is wrong with this batch.”

Foonmi tasted the food.

“It is fine.”

Tolu shook her head.

“Madam Ngozi never plated anything before tasting it first.”

Foonmi became annoyed.

“We do not have that luxury this evening.”

“But the customers will taste it.”

“Tolu, I said it is fine.”

Tolu stepped back.

She knew what was coming.

When the clients arrived, the food was served.

But within minutes, the complaints started.

One customer called Chidi aside.

“What happened to the food?”

Chidi tried to remain calm.

“Is there a problem, sir?”

“This is not what we ordered.”

Another customer joined them.

“The food tastes different.”

Chidi looked toward Foonmi.

She avoided his eyes.

The client demanded a full refund.

Chidi had no choice but to agree.

That evening, the kitchen was silent.

Tolu stood near the door.

Chidi looked at her.

“Say what you want to say.”

Tolu hesitated.

Then she finally spoke.

“Madam Ngozi should never have been let go, Chidi.”

Foonmi turned sharply.

“You are blaming me?”

Tolu shook her head.

“I am only saying what I know.”

She removed her apron.

“I cannot keep taking her blame.”

Chidi watched as Tolu walked away.

He looked around the kitchen.

For the first time, he began to understand something he had refused to accept.

Ngozi had not simply cooked the food.

She had been the heart of the business.

Meanwhile, across town, Ngozi stood beside Madam Adeze as another customer placed a large order.

“Ngozi, they asked for you by name,” Madam Adeze said.

Ngozi smiled.

“They did?”

“Yes.”

Ngozi looked at the growing list of orders.

For the first time in many years, she was no longer cooking to prove herself to a husband.

She was cooking because she knew what her hands could do.

And as the orders continued to come in, Madam Adeze began thinking about something bigger.

Something that could give Ngozi a place of her own.

Something that would soon put Ngozi's name on a door.

🌙 End of Chapter Three

🏪 Chapter Four – Ngozi's Kitchen

As the weeks passed, Ngozi's life began to look completely different.

She was no longer the woman walking along the road with one small bag, wondering where she would sleep.

She was now one of the busiest people at Madam Adeze's food stall.

Customers came early because they wanted her food.

Some customers even called ahead to make sure she would be there.

Madam Adeze noticed everything.

One evening, after they finished serving the last customer, Madam Adeze sat beside Ngozi.

“You have outgrown this place,” she said.

Ngozi looked surprised.

“What do you mean, madam?”

Madam Adeze smiled.

“You need a place of your own.”

Ngozi looked down at her hands.

For years, she had cooked in a business that carried Chidi's name.

She had never imagined putting her own name on a business.

“I don't have enough money to start something big,” she said.

“Who said it has to be big?” Madam Adeze asked.

Ngozi remained quiet.

Madam Adeze continued.

“Start with what you have. Grow slowly. But make sure it belongs to you.”

Ngozi smiled.

“What share would you want, madam?”

Madam Adeze shook her head.

“A small share only. This is your name, not mine.”

Ngozi looked at her in surprise.

“Then what will you put into it?”

Madam Adeze answered calmly.

“My money goes in the books. Your name goes on the door.”

Ngozi's eyes filled with tears.

She had once believed that losing Chidi meant losing everything.

Now someone was helping her build something that belonged completely to her.

They began making plans.

They found a small space that could be turned into a kitchen.

It was not perfect.

The walls needed work.

The kitchen needed equipment.

There were tables to buy and supplies to arrange.

But Ngozi did not complain.

Every small improvement made her feel more alive.

Then they chose a name.

Ngozi's Kitchen.

Ngozi stood outside the new place and stared at the sign.

Her name was finally on a door.

She touched the sign gently.

“Mama,” she whispered, “I wish you could see this.”

Madam Adeze stood beside her.

“Your mother is already part of it.”

Ngozi smiled.

“Because of her recipes?”

“Because of what she taught you.”

The first few days were not easy.

Ngozi had to learn how to manage everything herself.

She handled orders.

She checked ingredients.

She spoke with customers.

She supervised the cooking.

She also kept careful records.

She had learned one painful lesson from her marriage.

She would never again allow someone else to control everything she worked for.

Then the first big order came.

Sixty catering trays.

Ngozi looked at the order sheet twice.

“Sixty?” she asked.

Madam Adeze smiled.

“Can you handle it?”

Ngozi looked around the kitchen.

Her workers were already preparing the ingredients.

“We can.”

They worked from early morning until late afternoon.

Nobody rushed.

Nobody skipped the tasting.

Nobody ignored the small details.

Ngozi refused to compromise the quality of the food just to finish quickly.

At one point, one of the workers looked worried.

“Madam, if we keep this pace, we may have to refuse the next order.”

Ngozi nodded.

“Then we will refuse it.”

The worker looked surprised.

“But we need the money.”

Ngozi smiled.

“We need our reputation too.”

She remembered how quickly Chidi's business had begun losing customers.

She would not make the same mistake.

The sixty trays were delivered on time.

That evening, Ngozi received a message from the client.

“Thank you. Everyone loved the food.”

Ngozi smiled.

She had barely put her phone down when another order came in.

Then another.

The customers were not coming because of advertising.

They were coming because people were talking.

Word of mouth was spreading.

“Have you tried Ngozi's Kitchen?”

“Their food is excellent.”

“Ask for Ngozi.”

“They have never disappointed us.”

Meanwhile, Chidi's business was falling apart.

The cancellations continued.

Customers who had once trusted him stopped calling.

Workers began leaving.

Suppliers started asking for payments before delivering goods.

Even the biggest clients no longer trusted the business.

One afternoon, Chidi received a message that destroyed what was left of his confidence.

His biggest catering contract had been cancelled.

He sat alone in his office.

For the first time, there was nobody to blame.

Foonmi entered the room.

“Chidi, what happened?”

He looked at her.

“The biggest contract is gone.”

Foonmi became quiet.

“Maybe we can find another one.”

Chidi shook his head.

“There is no business left to find another one.”

He looked around the empty office.

“I gave up my marriage and destroyed my business for this.”

Foonmi looked offended.

“Are you blaming me?”

Chidi stood up.

“Do not blame me for choices you made yourself, Chidi.”

He stopped.

He knew she was right.

He had made his choices.

He had chosen to hide the truth.

He had chosen to send money to Foonmi for six years.

He had chosen to send Ngozi away.

And now he was living with the consequences.

He sat down again.

“I am asking what it bought me.”

Foonmi did not answer.

Chidi looked through the old business records.

Every successful event seemed to have one thing in common.

Ngozi's name appeared somewhere.

He remembered the long nights she spent cooking.

He remembered how customers praised her food.

He remembered how she could walk into a kitchen and immediately know what was wrong.

Then he whispered the words he had refused to accept.

“Everything changed the day she stopped cooking for me.”

Across town, Ngozi was standing inside her own kitchen.

The smell of fresh food filled the room.

Her workers were busy preparing another large order.

Madam Adeze walked in and smiled.

“You are doing well.”

Ngozi looked around.

“It still feels like a dream sometimes.”

Madam Adeze smiled.

“Then enjoy the dream while you build the next one.”

Ngozi laughed.

She knew there was still a long road ahead.

She wanted to grow.

She wanted to help other women.

She wanted to create something that could stand without her having to depend on anyone.

But for now, she looked at the sign outside her kitchen.

Ngozi's Kitchen.

Her own name.

Her own work.

Her own beginning.

And somewhere in the same city, the man who once told her that customers came for his name was slowly discovering just how valuable her hands had been.

🌙 End of Chapter Four

💔 Chapter Five – The Husband Who Came Back

Months had passed since Ngozi walked out of Chidi's house.

What started as a small kitchen had slowly become a successful business.

Ngozi's Kitchen was now known by many people in the city.

Customers came because they had heard about the food.

Others came because someone they trusted had recommended the place.

Ngozi worked hard every day, but she no longer felt like she was fighting to prove her worth.

She was building something that belonged to her.

Meanwhile, Chidi's catering business had completely collapsed.

The workers had left.

The suppliers stopped giving him credit.

The customers were gone.

The office that had once been full of activity was now almost empty.

One afternoon, Chidi sat alone at his desk, staring at the few documents that remained.

He had lost almost everything.

Then he remembered Ngozi.

He remembered the food.

He remembered the customers.

He remembered how the business had changed after she left.

For the first time, he wondered where she was and what she was doing.

Chidi decided to visit Madam Adeze.

He had heard that she knew Ngozi, and he hoped she might help him.

When he arrived at Madam Adeze's food stall, he tried to act confident.

“Good afternoon,” he said.

Madam Adeze looked at him.

“Good afternoon. What do you want?”

“I came to ask about Ngozi.”

Madam Adeze's face became serious.

“Why?”

“I need to speak with her.”

Before Madam Adeze could answer, a customer walked past them.

“We use Ngozi's Kitchen now. Their food has never disappointed us.”

Chidi turned around.

“Ngozi? Which Ngozi?”

Madam Adeze looked directly at him.

“This is the woman who owns it, Chidi.”

Chidi froze.

“What?”

Madam Adeze pointed toward the road.

“Ngozi's Kitchen.”

Chidi stared at the sign.

Then he saw the woman standing inside the kitchen.

His eyes widened.

“That is my wife's face.”

Madam Adeze folded her arms.

“Your wife?”

Chidi swallowed.

“She is my wife.”

Madam Adeze shook her head.

“She never once mentioned a husband.”

Chidi lowered his eyes.

“She would not have. Not after what I did.”

He stood outside for several minutes before finally entering.

Ngozi was checking an order when she noticed him.

Her hands stopped moving.

Chidi stood there quietly.

Ngozi looked at him without saying a word.

“Good afternoon,” he finally said.

Ngozi's voice was calm.

“Good afternoon. How may I help you, sir?”

Chidi looked at her.

“I am looking for Ngozi.”

Ngozi stared at him.

“You found her.”

Chidi's eyes moved around the kitchen.

He saw her name everywhere.

The sign.

The order books.

The business records.

Everything belonged to her.

“You built all this?” he asked.

“Yes.”

He smiled weakly.

“You always were good at cooking.”

Ngozi's expression did not change.

“Why are you here, Chidi?”

He looked down.

“I came to say I am sorry, Ngozi.”

Ngozi remained silent.

Chidi continued.

“I know I hurt you.”

“You did more than hurt me.”

He nodded.

“I know.”

“You threw me out of my own home.”

“I know.”

“You chose another woman after everything we built together.”

Chidi's eyes became wet.

“I made a terrible mistake.”

Ngozi looked away.

“Why are you really here?”

Chidi hesitated.

Then he finally said it.

“The business is finished, Ngozi. I have lost everything.”

Ngozi looked at him quietly.

“And?”

“I hoped you could come back and cook again.”

The room became silent.

Ngozi stared at him.

Then she understood.

His apology was not only about their marriage.

He needed what her hands could do.

He needed the same woman he had once told was not important.

Ngozi shook her head slowly.

“So the apology was for my hands, not my heart.”

“No, Ngozi.”

“Then why did you wait until you lost everything before remembering me?”

Chidi had no answer.

He stepped closer.

“Could we try again, Ngozi? As husband and wife?”

Ngozi looked at him for a long moment.

She remembered the night she had knelt beside her bed.

She remembered asking God for strength.

She remembered walking out of the house with one small bag.

She remembered the woman who fed her when she had nothing.

She remembered Madam Adeze opening her door.

She remembered the first day she cooked again.

She remembered the first customer who praised her food.

She remembered the day her name was placed on the door.

She had changed.

She was no longer the woman Chidi had sent away.

“I have already filed for our divorce, Chidi.”

Chidi's face changed.

“Ngozi, please.”

She shook her head.

“That door closed the night you chose her.”

“I was wrong.”

“I know.”

“Can't you forgive me?”

Ngozi looked at him calmly.

“I forgive what you did to me, Chidi.”

She paused.

“Forgiveness does not return me to the house where you erased me.”

Chidi lowered his head.

He finally understood that the woman standing before him was no longer waiting for him to choose her.

She had already chosen herself.

He turned toward the door.

Before leaving, he stopped.

“Thank you, Ngozi, for more than I deserve.”

Ngozi looked at him.

“Take care of yourself, Chidi. I mean that.”

Chidi nodded.

Then he walked away.

Ngozi watched him disappear.

She felt sadness.

She felt relief.

But most of all, she felt peace.

Madam Adeze came and stood beside her.

“Are you all right?”

Ngozi smiled.

“Yes, madam.”

She looked around her kitchen.

“I finally understand something.”

“What?” Madam Adeze asked.

Ngozi looked at the sign bearing her name.

“Sometimes the door that closes behind you is the same door that forces you to find your own.”

Madam Adeze smiled.

“And now that you have found yours?”

Ngozi looked at the busy kitchen.

“I want to leave it open for someone else.”

Madam Adeze looked at her with pride.

Neither of them knew that very soon, a young woman would walk through that door looking for the same chance Ngozi had once received.

🌙 End of Chapter Five

🌱 Chapter Six – A New Beginning

🌿 Moral of the Story

Sometimes losing what we thought we needed is the beginning of finding who we truly are. Never allow someone else's choices to make you forget your own worth.

When someone gives you a hand during your hardest moment, remember to become that helping hand for someone else when you rise again.
