🧵 Obioma the Tailor

🌍 African Folktales • 🕒 30 min read

🧵 Chapter One – The Tailor Everyone Needed

If a bride was preparing for her traditional marriage, Obioma was the woman for the job. If there was a special occasion in the community, everyone went to Obioma. In fact, some women joked that if your wardrobe did not contain at least one dress made by her, then perhaps you had been living under a rock.

Many years ago, in the peaceful town of Umudara, there lived a woman whose story remained on the lips of everyone for a very long time. People hardly remembered her real name anymore. Everyone simply called her Nwai Nkwakwa, which meant a woman who sewed clothes.

But for the sake of this story, we will call her Obioma.

Now, before you start wondering if she earned that name because she owned the only sewing machine in town, the answer is no. She earned it because she was unbelievably gifted.

Back then, things were not the way they are today. There were no fashion academies, no online tutorials, and no masterclasses promising to teach sewing in thirty days. Most women who knew how to sew had either learned from their mothers, their grandmothers, or through informal apprenticeship.

But Obioma belonged to another category entirely.

She was not simply taught. Sewing was a gift God had placed in her hands.

Obioma could look at someone's dress, study the way it folded around the body, and somehow figure out how to recreate it. Many women traveled from neighbouring villages just to meet her, and she never disappointed them.

Her stitches were always neat, her measurements were accurate, and her finishing was flawless. When Obioma completed a dress, people admired it as though they were looking at something created by a master designer.

Soon, her name became known far beyond Umudara.

Women brought her expensive lace, colourful Ankara, George wrappers, and beautiful fabrics for weddings, festivals, naming ceremonies, and other important occasions. Even women who had never met her personally had heard about her remarkable talent.

Whenever someone needed a special outfit, the advice was always the same.

“Take it to Obioma.”

At first, Obioma was grateful for every customer who entered her shop. She welcomed people warmly, worked hard, and took pride in seeing them leave happily with their finished clothes.

But as the years passed, something slowly began to change.

The more people praised Obioma, the more the praise entered her head. She began to believe that there was nobody in Umudara who could sew as well as she could.

And little by little, her talent began to change the way she treated people.

Sometimes, when someone greeted her, she would barely respond. At other times, she deliberately made customers wait, even when she had already finished their clothes.

Then she began increasing her prices.

A blouse she once sewed for five hundred naira suddenly cost one thousand.

When customers questioned the increase, Obioma always had an answer ready.

“If you think sewing is so easy, why don't you make it yourself?”

Some women laughed nervously and swallowed the insult because they desperately wanted Obioma to make their clothes. Others walked away angrily, only to return a few days later with the same fabric.

Obioma noticed this and became even more confident.

She began to believe that no matter how badly she treated people, they would always come back because they needed her.

But she was about to discover that a person's talent does not make them more important than everyone else.

One afternoon, the women of Umudara gathered for a meeting. The women's leader had called them together because many of them were tired of Obioma's sudden change in behaviour.

They loved her sewing, but they were no longer comfortable with the way she treated them.

One woman complained that Obioma had abandoned her fabric simply because she refused to pay extra.

Another woman said Obioma had told her that her fabric was too strong and would stress her scissors.

The women laughed at the excuse, but beneath the laughter was frustration.

Finally, they made a decision.

They would teach Obioma a lesson.

But they knew they could not confront her directly. If they accused her openly, she would become suspicious and might refuse to help them altogether.

So the women's leader came up with a different plan.

They would learn from Obioma herself.

A few women were carefully chosen for the mission. Whenever one of them went to Obioma's shop to take measurements or drop off fabric, she would no longer hurry home.

Instead, she would find an excuse to remain there.

One woman would pretend she was waiting for her sister. Another would say her husband had taken the only umbrella, so she had to wait for the rain to stop. Sometimes, one would simply smile and say,

“Let me keep you company.”

Obioma thought nothing of it.

After all, she was the most sought-after tailor in Umudara. Why would anyone not want to spend a little extra time around her?

She assumed the women were trying to get into her good books or hoping she would give their clothes extra attention.

She had no idea that while she proudly worked behind her sewing machine, the women were quietly watching every move she made.

One woman noticed the way Obioma ironed certain seams before joining the next pieces.

Another carefully memorized how she marked her chalk lines before cutting.

They watched how she handled different fabrics, how she measured, how she joined pieces, and how she gave her clothes that neat finishing everyone admired.

Little by little, knowledge was changing hands.

And while Obioma believed her position in Umudara was untouchable, the women were quietly preparing to prove her wrong.

🌙 End of Chapter One

👑 Chapter Two – When Talent Became Pride

Obioma's reputation continued to grow, and with every new customer came another reason for her to believe that she was better than everyone around her.

Women from neighbouring villages still came to Umudara with their fabrics, especially when they had an important ceremony approaching. A bride's family could spend days searching for the right material, but once they found it, they already knew who they wanted to sew it.

“Take it to Obioma,” they would say.

Obioma enjoyed hearing her name mentioned everywhere. She loved the attention, the compliments, and the way people admired the clothes that came out of her shop.

Unfortunately, the praise slowly changed her.

She stopped seeing her customers as the people who had helped build her business. Instead, she began treating them as though they were lucky simply to have access to her sewing machine.

If a woman arrived early in the morning, Obioma might tell her to wait until she finished whatever she was doing. Sometimes she would continue chatting with a friend while her customer stood nearby holding a carefully folded piece of fabric.

When the woman finally asked when her measurements would be taken, Obioma would look up and say,

“Can't you see I'm busy?”

The woman would quietly apologize and wait.

Obioma had become so confident in her position that she no longer worried about losing customers.

She knew that many of them would return.

And that belief made her even more careless.

Her prices began changing almost as quickly as her attitude. A dress that had once been affordable suddenly cost much more. When customers complained, Obioma never apologized.

“If you think my price is too much,” she would tell them, “find another tailor.”

The words hurt, but most women endured them because they wanted Obioma's work.

However, the women of Umudara eventually reached their limit.

One afternoon, the women's leader called a meeting. The women gathered together and began sharing their experiences.

One woman complained that Obioma had abandoned her fabric because she refused to pay extra.

Another said Obioma had complained that her fabric was too strong and would stress her scissors.

The women laughed at the excuse, but nobody was really amused.

They had tolerated Obioma for long enough.

“She is talented,” one woman admitted, “but that does not give her the right to insult us.”

Another woman nodded.

“We cannot continue like this.”

The women's leader listened carefully before speaking.

“We are not going to fight her,” she said. “And we are not going to destroy her business.”

The women looked at her curiously.

“Then what are we going to do?”

The leader smiled.

“We are going to learn.”

That was when the plan began.

A few women were chosen to visit Obioma's shop regularly. Whenever they had fabric to drop off or measurements to take, they would deliberately remain there longer than usual.

One would say she was waiting for her sister.

Another would claim that the rain had started and she had nowhere else to go.

Sometimes, one would simply smile and tell Obioma,

“Let me keep you company.”

Obioma never suspected anything.

In her mind, the women were simply trying to get on her good side.

While she worked, they watched.

They paid attention to how she took measurements. They observed how she marked her chalk lines before cutting. They studied the way she handled different fabrics and the order in which she joined each piece.

One woman noticed that Obioma always ironed certain seams before joining the next pieces.

Another carefully memorized the way she positioned her fabric before cutting.

They watched everything.

Obioma, completely unaware of their intentions, continued sewing and talking proudly about her work.

“People think sewing is easy,” she would say. “They don't know what it takes to make clothes like mine.”

The women would smile and nod.

But the moment they left her shop, they carried what they had learned with them.

Every three days, they gathered secretly beneath an old Udala tree where very few people paid attention to them.

The tree became their classroom.

One woman would explain how Obioma took measurements.

Another would demonstrate the way she had seen Obioma cut a particular piece of fabric.

Those who had learned sewing from their mothers and grandmothers already understood the basics. Now they were simply filling in the missing pieces.

At first, their clothes were not perfect.

Some stitches were uneven. Some measurements were wrong. Some pieces did not fit together properly.

But they refused to give up.

“If Obioma learned, we can learn too,” one of them said.

The women began contributing whatever they could.

Some brought thread. Others donated scissors, needles, and fabric. Those who had a little money contributed what they could.

Eventually, they had enough to buy something that would change everything.

A sewing machine.

The machine became the busiest object in the village.

Morning, afternoon, and evening, the women took turns practicing. Whenever one woman finished, another immediately took her place.

Mistakes were no longer reasons to stop. If someone made an error, she simply tried again.

Weeks passed.

Then months.

Slowly, the results began to show.

The women who had once struggled to sew even simple clothes were now producing dresses that were genuinely beautiful.

Their confidence grew with every successful outfit.

Meanwhile, Obioma remained completely unaware of what was happening.

She continued sitting comfortably in her shop, believing that she was still the only person in Umudara who could produce truly beautiful clothes.

Then news spread that the town's greatest festival was approaching.

It was a celebration that came only once every two years.

Everyone knew what that meant.

New wrappers would appear. Beautiful blouses would be sewn. Expensive lace, Ankara, George fabrics, and colourful beads would fill the market.

Obioma smiled when she heard the news.

“This festival will make me rich,” she thought.

She had no idea that the very women she had looked down on were preparing for the biggest surprise of her life.

🌿 Moral of the Story

Talent is a gift, but pride can destroy what talent builds. We should never use our abilities to look down on others or take advantage of them. True greatness comes when we use our gifts to help others and treat everyone with respect.
