🧵 Obioma the Tailor

🌍 African Folktales • 🕒 30 min read

🧵 Chapter One – The Tailor Everyone Needed

If a bride was preparing for her traditional marriage, Obioma was the woman for the job. If there was a special occasion in the community, everyone went to Obioma. In fact, some women joked that if your wardrobe did not contain at least one dress made by her, then perhaps you had been living under a rock.

Many years ago, in the peaceful town of Umudara, there lived a woman whose story remained on the lips of everyone for a very long time. People hardly remembered her real name anymore. Everyone simply called her Nwai Nkwakwa, which meant a woman who sewed clothes.

But for the sake of this story, we will call her Obioma.

Now, before you start wondering if she earned that name because she owned the only sewing machine in town, the answer is no. She earned it because she was unbelievably gifted.

Back then, things were not the way they are today. There were no fashion academies, no online tutorials, and no masterclasses promising to teach sewing in thirty days. Most women who knew how to sew had either learned from their mothers, their grandmothers, or through informal apprenticeship.

But Obioma belonged to another category entirely.

She was not simply taught. Sewing was a gift God had placed in her hands.

Obioma could look at someone's dress, study the way it folded around the body, and somehow figure out how to recreate it. Many women traveled from neighbouring villages just to meet her, and she never disappointed them.

Her stitches were always neat, her measurements were accurate, and her finishing was flawless. When Obioma completed a dress, people admired it as though they were looking at something created by a master designer.

Soon, her name became known far beyond Umudara.

Women brought her expensive lace, colourful Ankara, George wrappers, and beautiful fabrics for weddings, festivals, naming ceremonies, and other important occasions. Even women who had never met her personally had heard about her remarkable talent.

Whenever someone needed a special outfit, the advice was always the same.

“Take it to Obioma.”

At first, Obioma was grateful for every customer who entered her shop. She welcomed people warmly, worked hard, and took pride in seeing them leave happily with their finished clothes.

But as the years passed, something slowly began to change.

The more people praised Obioma, the more the praise entered her head. She began to believe that there was nobody in Umudara who could sew as well as she could.

And little by little, her talent began to change the way she treated people.

Sometimes, when someone greeted her, she would barely respond. At other times, she deliberately made customers wait, even when she had already finished their clothes.

Then she began increasing her prices.

A blouse she once sewed for five hundred naira suddenly cost one thousand.

When customers questioned the increase, Obioma always had an answer ready.

“If you think sewing is so easy, why don't you make it yourself?”

Some women laughed nervously and swallowed the insult because they desperately wanted Obioma to make their clothes. Others walked away angrily, only to return a few days later with the same fabric.

Obioma noticed this and became even more confident.

She began to believe that no matter how badly she treated people, they would always come back because they needed her.

But she was about to discover that a person's talent does not make them more important than everyone else.

One afternoon, the women of Umudara gathered for a meeting. The women's leader had called them together because many of them were tired of Obioma's sudden change in behaviour.

They loved her sewing, but they were no longer comfortable with the way she treated them.

One woman complained that Obioma had abandoned her fabric simply because she refused to pay extra.

Another woman said Obioma had told her that her fabric was too strong and would stress her scissors.

The women laughed at the excuse, but beneath the laughter was frustration.

Finally, they made a decision.

They would teach Obioma a lesson.

But they knew they could not confront her directly. If they accused her openly, she would become suspicious and might refuse to help them altogether.

So the women's leader came up with a different plan.

They would learn from Obioma herself.

A few women were carefully chosen for the mission. Whenever one of them went to Obioma's shop to take measurements or drop off fabric, she would no longer hurry home.

Instead, she would find an excuse to remain there.

One woman would pretend she was waiting for her sister. Another would say her husband had taken the only umbrella, so she had to wait for the rain to stop. Sometimes, one would simply smile and say,

“Let me keep you company.”

Obioma thought nothing of it.

After all, she was the most sought-after tailor in Umudara. Why would anyone not want to spend a little extra time around her?

She assumed the women were trying to get into her good books or hoping she would give their clothes extra attention.

She had no idea that while she proudly worked behind her sewing machine, the women were quietly watching every move she made.

One woman noticed the way Obioma ironed certain seams before joining the next pieces.

Another carefully memorized how she marked her chalk lines before cutting.

They watched how she handled different fabrics, how she measured, how she joined pieces, and how she gave her clothes that neat finishing everyone admired.

Little by little, knowledge was changing hands.

And while Obioma believed her position in Umudara was untouchable, the women were quietly preparing to prove her wrong.

🌙 End of Chapter One

👑 Chapter Two – When Talent Became Pride

Obioma's reputation continued to grow, and with every new customer came another reason for her to believe that she was better than everyone around her.

Women from neighbouring villages still came to Umudara with their fabrics, especially when they had an important ceremony approaching. A bride's family could spend days searching for the right material, but once they found it, they already knew who they wanted to sew it.

“Take it to Obioma,” they would say.

Obioma enjoyed hearing her name mentioned everywhere. She loved the attention, the compliments, and the way people admired the clothes that came out of her shop.

Unfortunately, the praise slowly changed her.

She stopped seeing her customers as the people who had helped build her business. Instead, she began treating them as though they were lucky simply to have access to her sewing machine.

If a woman arrived early in the morning, Obioma might tell her to wait until she finished whatever she was doing. Sometimes she would continue chatting with a friend while her customer stood nearby holding a carefully folded piece of fabric.

When the woman finally asked when her measurements would be taken, Obioma would look up and say,

“Can't you see I'm busy?”

The woman would quietly apologize and wait.

Obioma had become so confident in her position that she no longer worried about losing customers.

She knew that many of them would return.

And that belief made her even more careless.

Her prices began changing almost as quickly as her attitude. A dress that had once been affordable suddenly cost much more. When customers complained, Obioma never apologized.

“If you think my price is too much,” she would tell them, “find another tailor.”

The words hurt, but most women endured them because they wanted Obioma's work.

However, the women of Umudara eventually reached their limit.

One afternoon, the women's leader called a meeting. The women gathered together and began sharing their experiences.

One woman complained that Obioma had abandoned her fabric because she refused to pay extra.

Another said Obioma had complained that her fabric was too strong and would stress her scissors.

The women laughed at the excuse, but nobody was really amused.

They had tolerated Obioma for long enough.

“She is talented,” one woman admitted, “but that does not give her the right to insult us.”

Another woman nodded.

“We cannot continue like this.”

The women's leader listened carefully before speaking.

“We are not going to fight her,” she said. “And we are not going to destroy her business.”

The women looked at her curiously.

“Then what are we going to do?”

The leader smiled.

“We are going to learn.”

That was when the plan began.

A few women were chosen to visit Obioma's shop regularly. Whenever they had fabric to drop off or measurements to take, they would deliberately remain there longer than usual.

One would say she was waiting for her sister.

Another would claim that the rain had started and she had nowhere else to go.

Sometimes, one would simply smile and tell Obioma,

“Let me keep you company.”

Obioma never suspected anything.

In her mind, the women were simply trying to get on her good side.

While she worked, they watched.

They paid attention to how she took measurements. They observed how she marked her chalk lines before cutting. They studied the way she handled different fabrics and the order in which she joined each piece.

One woman noticed that Obioma always ironed certain seams before joining the next pieces.

Another carefully memorized the way she positioned her fabric before cutting.

They watched everything.

Obioma, completely unaware of their intentions, continued sewing and talking proudly about her work.

“People think sewing is easy,” she would say. “They don't know what it takes to make clothes like mine.”

The women would smile and nod.

But the moment they left her shop, they carried what they had learned with them.

Every three days, they gathered secretly beneath an old Udala tree where very few people paid attention to them.

The tree became their classroom.

One woman would explain how Obioma took measurements.

Another would demonstrate the way she had seen Obioma cut a particular piece of fabric.

Those who had learned sewing from their mothers and grandmothers already understood the basics. Now they were simply filling in the missing pieces.

At first, their clothes were not perfect.

Some stitches were uneven. Some measurements were wrong. Some pieces did not fit together properly.

But they refused to give up.

“If Obioma learned, we can learn too,” one of them said.

The women began contributing whatever they could.

Some brought thread. Others donated scissors, needles, and fabric. Those who had a little money contributed what they could.

Eventually, they had enough to buy something that would change everything.

A sewing machine.

The machine became the busiest object in the village.

Morning, afternoon, and evening, the women took turns practicing. Whenever one woman finished, another immediately took her place.

Mistakes were no longer reasons to stop. If someone made an error, she simply tried again.

Weeks passed.

Then months.

Slowly, the results began to show.

The women who had once struggled to sew even simple clothes were now producing dresses that were genuinely beautiful.

Their confidence grew with every successful outfit.

Meanwhile, Obioma remained completely unaware of what was happening.

She continued sitting comfortably in her shop, believing that she was still the only person in Umudara who could produce truly beautiful clothes.

Then news spread that the town's greatest festival was approaching.

It was a celebration that came only once every two years.

Everyone knew what that meant.

New wrappers would appear. Beautiful blouses would be sewn. Expensive lace, Ankara, George fabrics, and colourful beads would fill the market.

Obioma smiled when she heard the news.

“This festival will make me rich,” she thought.

She had no idea that the very women she had looked down on were preparing for the biggest surprise of her life.

🌙 End of Chapter Two

🤫 Chapter Three – The Women's Secret

The women of Umudara had started with very little, but they were determined to learn. Every three days, they returned to the old Udala tree with whatever new thing they had discovered.

One woman would explain how Obioma had handled a particular fabric. Another would show the others how she had seen Obioma mark her measurements before cutting. Those who already had some knowledge of sewing helped the others understand what they had observed.

They were not trying to become perfect overnight. They simply kept practicing.

Their sewing machine became the centre of their little training group. In the morning, one woman would practice. In the afternoon, another would take her turn. By evening, someone else would sit down and continue.

Whenever they made a mistake, they corrected it and tried again.

Slowly, the women became better.

Weeks turned into months.

The rough stitches they once produced became neater. Their measurements became more accurate. Their confidence grew with every dress they completed.

Soon, they began helping one another make clothes for family members and friends.

Nobody outside their group understood how far they had come.

And most importantly, Obioma had no idea.

Whenever the women came into her shop and stayed for a while, she assumed they were there because they wanted her attention.

Sometimes she would continue sewing while they watched quietly.

She never imagined that every movement of her hands was being carefully remembered.

Obioma had become so used to being admired that she never stopped to wonder why these women suddenly seemed so interested in watching her work.

She thought they respected her talent.

She did not realize they were learning from it.

Then the news everyone had been waiting for finally arrived.

The great festival of Umudara was approaching.

The festival was held only once every two years, and it was one of the biggest celebrations in the town. People began preparing long before the actual day.

Women started bringing out their finest fabrics. Beautiful Ankara, George, lace, velvet, and other colourful materials appeared everywhere. New wrappers were bought, beads were prepared, and women began planning the hairstyles and outfits they would wear.

For Obioma, the approaching festival meant one thing.

Business.

She expected her shop to become crowded with customers.

She imagined herself working from morning until night, taking measurements, cutting fabrics, sewing dresses, and collecting payment.

She even began calculating how much money she would make.

“This festival will be very good for me,” she told herself.

But while Obioma was counting the money she had not yet earned, the women were making their own plans.

They had decided that they would not take their festival fabrics to Obioma.

Instead, they would trust the new tailors they had trained among themselves.

It was not because they suddenly hated Obioma.

They simply wanted to prove that they could learn.

They wanted to know that they did not have to depend on one person forever.

So as the festival drew closer, women quietly began taking their fabrics to the new tailors.

Some were nervous.

“What if the sewing is not as perfect as Obioma's?” one woman asked.

Another woman smiled.

“Then we will improve. We have to start somewhere.”

They were willing to accept a few imperfect stitches because what mattered most to them was their dignity.

Meanwhile, Obioma sat in her shop waiting for the usual flood of customers.

At first, she did not notice anything unusual.

Then one day passed.

Then another.

Still, hardly anyone came.

Obioma began wondering what was happening.

She looked around her quiet shop and tried to convince herself that the customers would soon arrive.

But the days continued passing.

Finally, she went out to ask questions.

She spoke to an onion seller in the market and casually asked why she had not seen many women bringing fabrics to her shop.

The onion seller looked at her for a moment before answering.

“The women are taking their clothes to the new tailors.”

Obioma laughed.

“New tailors?”

“Yes,” the woman replied.

Obioma shook her head.

She could not believe it.

“Those people cannot sew like me.”

She dismissed them as amateurs and returned to her shop.

But something had already changed in Umudara.

The women she once believed could never do without her had learned enough to stand on their own.

And now Obioma was about to see the result with her own eyes.

Determined to prove that she was still the finest tailor in the town, Obioma decided to sew herself a magnificent outfit for the festival.

She wanted everyone to see her dress and remember exactly who the best tailor in Umudara was.

She had no idea that the festival would soon become the moment she would never forget.

🌙 End of Chapter Three

🎉 Chapter Four – The Festival That Shocked Obioma

The long-awaited festival finally arrived.

From the early hours of the morning, Umudara was already alive with excitement. Women dressed in their finest wrappers and blouses. Beautiful beads decorated their necks, wrists, and heads. Colourful fabrics could be seen everywhere, and the whole town seemed brighter than usual.

Obioma dressed carefully in the magnificent outfit she had made for herself.

She had spent a great deal of time making it because she wanted everyone to notice her work. She believed that once the women saw her outfit, they would remember that she was still the finest tailor in Umudara.

As she walked towards the festival ground, Obioma was already imagining the compliments she would receive.

But something unexpected happened.

Instead of people gathering around her, Obioma began noticing beautiful outfits everywhere she looked.

One woman passed wearing a neatly fitted blouse and wrapper.

Another appeared in a beautifully designed Ankara outfit.

Then another woman walked past in a colourful dress with a finishing that immediately caught Obioma's attention.

Obioma looked closely.

She recognized the sewing techniques.

The way the seams had been handled.

The careful measurements.

The neat finishing.

These were things she had believed only she could do properly.

She looked around again.

There were beautiful clothes everywhere.

The women had not produced perfect clothes overnight, but they had improved tremendously. Their outfits showed that they had learned, practiced, corrected their mistakes, and helped one another.

Obioma's smile slowly disappeared.

She had expected to see poorly made dresses from amateurs.

Instead, she was seeing the result of months of hard work.

For the first time, Obioma understood why her shop had become so quiet.

The women had learned from her.

They had taken what they observed in her shop, practiced it under the old Udala tree, and taught one another until they became capable of sewing their own clothes.

The festival continued, but Obioma no longer enjoyed it the way she had imagined.

She kept looking at the women and thinking about how she had treated them.

She remembered the customers she had made to wait.

She remembered the people she had insulted.

She remembered the times she had increased her prices and spoken to people as though they were beneath her.

She remembered saying,

“If you think sewing is so easy, why don't you make it yourself?”

Now they had made it themselves.

After the festival, Obioma went to meet the women's leader.

She did not go there to argue.

She went because she finally wanted to understand.

“Why did you do this?” Obioma asked.

The women's leader looked at her calmly.

“We never wanted to destroy your business,” she said.

Obioma listened.

“We only wanted you to understand how you made us feel.”

Those words touched Obioma deeply.

The leader continued.

“You are gifted. Nobody doubts that. Your talent is real, and we have always admired your work. But every time we entered your shop, you made us feel small.”

Obioma lowered her head.

For the first time, she saw herself through the eyes of the people she had treated badly.

Her talent had never been the problem.

Her pride was.

She remembered the people who had brought her their fabrics when she was still building her reputation. She remembered the customers who had recommended her to others. She remembered how their support had helped make her business successful.

Yet somewhere along the way, she had forgotten them.

“I am sorry,” Obioma finally said.

The words were simple, but they were sincere.

The women could see that she was no longer speaking as the proud tailor who believed nobody could replace her.

She was speaking as a woman who had finally learned a difficult lesson.

The women's leader smiled.

“We never wanted to replace you, Obioma.”

Obioma looked at the women around her.

“We only wanted you to understand that your talent should not make other people feel worthless.”

Obioma nodded.

She had nothing more to say.

The lesson had already been learned.

But what happened next would change not only Obioma's life, but the future of tailoring in Umudara.

🌙 End of Chapter Four

❤️ Chapter Five – The Tailor Who Learned Her Lesson

After the festival, Obioma spent a long time thinking about everything that had happened.

For years, she had believed that her talent made her more important than the people around her. She had forgotten that the same customers she treated badly were the people who had helped her business grow.

The women had never wanted to destroy her.

They only wanted her to understand how painful it was to be treated as though they were not good enough.

Obioma finally accepted the truth.

She was talented, but she was not the only person who could learn.

The women had learned from watching her work, and with patience, practice, and cooperation, they had become good tailors themselves.

The next time the women came to her shop, Obioma treated them differently.

There was no arrogance in her voice.

There were no unnecessary insults.

She welcomed them and spoke to them with respect.

“I am sorry for the way I treated you,” she told them. “I allowed pride to enter my heart.”

The women accepted her apology.

They knew that Obioma was genuinely sorry.

From that day forward, things began to change in Umudara.

Obioma continued sewing, especially for customers who wanted special designs and outfits for important occasions. But she no longer behaved as though every other person was beneath her.

The younger tailors also continued working.

Instead of competing with one another, they helped one another. When one tailor received more work than she could handle, another would assist her.

Obioma also became willing to share what she knew.

She no longer guarded every sewing technique as though teaching someone else would reduce her own talent.

In fact, she discovered something she had never understood before.

Teaching someone did not make her less talented.

It made her knowledge live beyond her own hands.

The women began getting regular sewing jobs, while Obioma continued receiving special requests from people who wanted her particular style and experience.

There was now enough work for everyone.

Umudara no longer depended on one tailor.

The town had many good hands.

Whenever people talked about sewing, they no longer said only,

“Take it to Obioma.”

They could now mention several women who had learned, practiced, and built their own skills.

Obioma was happy about it.

She had finally understood that being the best did not mean making everyone else feel small.

Years later, whenever younger women came to learn sewing from her, Obioma would tell them about the lesson she had learned the hard way.

“Talent is a gift,” she would say, “but if you allow pride to control you, the same talent that lifted you up can become the reason people turn away from you.”

The women of Umudara never forgot what had happened beneath the old Udala tree.

And Obioma never forgot the day she walked into the festival expecting to see amateurs, only to discover that the people she had looked down on had quietly learned from her.

She had started as the only tailor everyone needed.

She ended up becoming something even more valuable.

A tailor who understood the importance of people.

🌿 Moral of the Story

Talent is a gift, but pride can destroy what talent builds. No matter how skilled or successful you become, always treat people with respect. When you share your knowledge and help others grow, your success becomes even greater.
