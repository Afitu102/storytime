💔 Rejected by Her Father

🌍 African Folktales • 🕒 30 min read

💔 Chapter One – The Child Her Father Rejected

The night Naledi was born, the small house in a quiet township outside Polokwane was filled with the sound of a newborn baby crying.

Her mother, Thandiwe, held her close against her chest and stared at her tiny face. She had been waiting for this moment for nine long months. Even though the delivery had left her exhausted, the smile on her face refused to disappear.

“My little girl,” she whispered. “You are finally here.”

The baby had soft features, tiny fingers, and a head covered with a small amount of dark hair. But there was something about her appearance that made her different from the other babies Thandiwe had seen in their neighbourhood.

Her skin was remarkably light.

Thandiwe noticed it immediately, but it did not matter to her. She kissed her daughter's forehead and wrapped the blanket more tightly around her.

Outside the clinic, the early morning air was cold. Minibus taxis were already beginning to move along the road, and the first sounds of the township were coming alive. Women walked toward the taxi rank with shopping bags in their hands while men prepared for another day of work.

Thandiwe's husband, Mandla, arrived shortly after sunrise.

He had spent the entire night waiting for news about his wife and child. When he entered the room, his face showed relief.

“Is she here?” he asked.

Thandiwe smiled.

“She is here.”

Mandla stepped closer.

For a moment, he looked at his wife. Then he looked at the baby in her arms.

His smile disappeared.

He stared at the child for several seconds without saying a word.

Thandiwe noticed the change in his face.

“What is wrong?” she asked.

Mandla pointed at the baby.

“Whose child is that?”

Thandiwe frowned.

“Your daughter.”

Mandla shook his head.

“That is not my child.”

The words struck Thandiwe harder than the pain she had felt during childbirth.

She looked down at the baby and then back at her husband.

“What are you saying?”

Mandla's voice became louder.

“Look at her, Thandiwe. Look at her properly. Does this child look like me?”

The room became quiet.

Thandiwe pulled the baby closer.

“She is a newborn. Why are you speaking like this?”

Mandla turned away and rubbed his face.

“I have never seen a child like this in my family.”

“You think I betrayed you?”

He did not answer.

That silence was enough.

Tears gathered in Thandiwe's eyes.

She had spent years loving this man. She had shared his struggles, cooked for him, stood beside him when money was scarce, and waited patiently for the day they would have a child together.

Now, barely an hour after giving birth, her husband was looking at their daughter as if she were a stranger.

Mandla finally spoke.

“Tell me the truth. Who is the father?”

Thandiwe's lips trembled.

“You are.”

“I don't believe you.”

The baby began crying.

Thandiwe immediately rocked her gently.

“Don't listen to him, my baby,” she whispered. “Your mother knows the truth.”

Mandla walked toward the door.

“I am not taking that child home.”

Thandiwe looked up sharply.

“Mandla!”

He stopped but refused to turn around.

“My family will laugh at me. The whole neighbourhood will talk. You expect me to walk around with a child who looks nothing like me?”

Thandiwe's voice broke.

“She is your daughter.”

Mandla opened the door.

“Then raise her yourself.”

He walked out.

Thandiwe watched him disappear down the corridor, carrying the weight of a decision that would change their family forever.

She looked at the baby in her arms.

Naledi had stopped crying.

Her tiny eyes were open, looking directly at her mother.

Thandiwe kissed her cheek.

“They may reject you,” she whispered, “but I will never reject you.”

When they returned home days later, the atmosphere was completely different from what Thandiwe had imagined.

Mandla refused to sleep in the same room with her. He barely spoke to her, and whenever Naledi cried, he left the house.

Soon, people in the neighbourhood began talking.

Some women whispered when Thandiwe passed them. Others stared at Naledi whenever she carried her outside.

One afternoon, Thandiwe overheard two women speaking near a small grocery shop.

“That baby is very strange,” one woman said.

“The father says the child is not his.”

“Maybe Thandiwe knows something she is not telling people.”

Thandiwe walked past them without responding.

She had learned that day that gossip could be louder than truth.

At home, Mandla became colder.

One evening, while Thandiwe was preparing supper, he entered the room and found Naledi sleeping on a blanket.

He stared at the child for a long time.

Then something strange happened.

Naledi opened her eyes.

For a brief moment, father and daughter looked directly at each other.

Mandla's expression changed.

There was something familiar in the baby's face.

Something he had seen before.

But before he could understand what it was, Naledi smiled.

Mandla quickly looked away.

“I still don't accept her,” he said.

Thandiwe turned around.

“Then one day, Mandla, you will have to explain to her why you rejected her before she was even old enough to know your name.”

He walked away without answering.

That night, while Thandiwe slept beside her daughter, Mandla sat alone outside the house.

He looked at an old photograph of himself as a young man.

His eyes remained fixed on the picture.

Then he looked toward the room where Naledi was sleeping.

For the first time, a frightening thought entered his mind.

What if the child really was his?

And if she was, why did she look so different?

Mandla had no idea that the answer would eventually force him to face a truth about his own family that nobody had spoken about for generations.

🌙 End of Chapter One

😭 Chapter Two – The Girl Everyone Questioned

The months that followed Naledi’s birth were not easy for Thandiwe. Every time she stepped outside with her daughter, she could feel people looking at them. Some looked out of curiosity. Others looked because they had already heard Mandla’s accusation.

In the township, news travelled faster than a taxi leaving the rank.

People knew that Mandla had rejected his own daughter.

They also knew why.

Naledi’s skin was much lighter than Thandiwe’s, and her appearance was different from the other children in the neighbourhood. To some people, that difference was enough to create a story.

Thandiwe heard the whispers but refused to let them change the way she treated her child.

Every morning, she bathed Naledi, dressed her carefully, tied a little ribbon around her hair and carried her outside to enjoy the fresh air.

When people stared, Thandiwe smiled.

When they whispered, she kept walking.

And when someone asked whether Mandla had finally accepted the child, she simply replied, “My daughter does not need his permission to be loved.”

Mandla, however, remained distant.

He provided money for the household when he could, but he never bought anything for Naledi. He did not celebrate when she smiled for the first time. He did not attend the small gathering Thandiwe arranged when the baby started crawling.

He treated her as though she were a guest who had overstayed her welcome.

But there was one thing Mandla could not escape.

Whenever he passed Naledi, she watched him.

She was too young to understand his rejection. To her, he was simply the tall man who lived in the same house.

Sometimes she would stretch her little hands toward him.

Mandla would turn away.

Thandiwe noticed every time.

It broke her heart, but she never allowed Naledi to see her sadness.

“You are loved,” she would tell her daughter before putting her to sleep. “Never let anybody make you believe otherwise.”

When Naledi turned three, something unexpected began to happen.

Her face started changing.

Her features became sharper, her eyes brighter, and a small smile appeared whenever she was excited. She was a beautiful child, but what caught people's attention was not only her beauty.

There was something strangely familiar about her face.

One afternoon, Thandiwe was buying vegetables at a busy market when an elderly woman who had known Mandla for years suddenly stopped beside them.

She stared at Naledi.

Then she looked at Thandiwe.

Then back at Naledi.

“Wait,” the woman said.

Thandiwe smiled politely.

“Is something wrong?”

The woman shook her head slowly.

“That child has Mandla’s eyes.”

Thandiwe's smile disappeared.

The woman continued staring at Naledi.

“And that little nose… I have seen it before.”

Thandiwe said nothing.

The woman leaned closer.

“When Mandla was a boy, he had exactly that expression whenever he was angry.”

Thandiwe looked down at her daughter.

For the first time, she began noticing the resemblance herself.

She had always known Naledi was her child. She had never doubted her own truth. But now she could see small pieces of Mandla appearing in the girl's face.

That evening, when Mandla came home from work, Thandiwe watched him carefully.

Naledi was sitting on the floor playing with a wooden toy.

Mandla walked past her.

Naledi looked up.

For a moment, their faces carried almost the same expression.

Mandla stopped.

He looked at his daughter.

Then he quickly walked into the bedroom.

Thandiwe followed him.

“What happened?”

“Nothing.”

“You saw her.”

Mandla remained silent.

“You saw the resemblance,” Thandiwe said.

Mandla turned toward her.

“Do not start this again.”

“I'm not starting anything. I am asking you to look at your daughter.”

“I have looked at her.”

“Then look again.”

Mandla's face tightened.

He walked out of the room.

But that night, he took the old photograph of himself that he had been keeping for years and studied it under the light.

It was a picture of him at about five years old.

The same eyes.

The same shape of the nose.

The same slight curve around the mouth.

Mandla stared at the photograph for a long time.

His anger began turning into something he did not want to admit.

Doubt.

The following morning, he took the photograph to an old family friend who had known his parents for decades.

The man looked at the photograph and laughed.

“You still have this?”

Mandla nodded.

Then he showed him a picture of Naledi.

The man's smile disappeared.

He looked at the two photographs several times.

“Where did you get this child?”

Mandla swallowed.

“She is my daughter.”

The old man looked at him carefully.

“Then why are you asking me?”

Mandla had no answer.

The old man handed the photographs back.

“There are things about your family you were never told.”

Mandla stared at him.

“What things?”

The old man sighed.

“Your grandmother had a child who looked very different from the rest of the family. The family kept it quiet because they were ashamed of her appearance.”

Mandla's eyes widened.

“What happened to her?”

“She was sent away when she was young.”

“Where?”

The old man looked away.

“That is the part nobody wanted to talk about.”

Mandla returned home with more questions than answers.

For the first time since Naledi's birth, he stood outside the house and watched his daughter playing in the yard.

She was laughing.

Thandiwe was hanging clothes on the washing line nearby.

Naledi looked up and saw him.

Instead of running away, she smiled.

“Papa!”

The word caught Mandla completely off guard.

He had never taught her to call him that.

Thandiwe looked at him.

Mandla looked at Naledi.

For several seconds, nobody spoke.

Then Naledi stretched her arms toward him.

Mandla took one step forward.

But before he could reach her, a voice came from the gate.

“Mandla!”

He turned around.

The old family friend was standing outside.

His face was serious.

“I found the name of the woman your grandmother sent away,” he said.

Mandla's expression changed.

The man looked at Naledi.

“She may be the reason your daughter looks the way she does.”

Mandla stepped toward the gate.

“Who was she?”

The old man lowered his voice.

“She was your grandmother's daughter… and she never left this country.”
