🙏🏿 The Praying Mother in Azabre

🌍 African Folktales • 🕒 35 min read

🙏🏿 Chapter One – A Mother's Prayer

In the village of Azabre, there lived a woman named Amara.

Amara was known by almost everyone in the village.

Not because she was rich.

Not because she owned the biggest farm.

People knew her because of one thing.

She prayed for her son every day.

Her son was called Chukwudi.

He was a quiet young boy with a gentle heart.

Every morning before Chukwudi left the house, Amara would place her hand on his head.

"May your feet take you to good places."

"May your eyes see what is good."

"May your hands never be used to hurt another person."

"And may you always remember where you come from."

Chukwudi would smile.

"Amen, Mama."

Then he would leave for the day's work.

Amara did not pray only when something was wrong.

She prayed when things were good.

She prayed when the sun was bright.

She prayed when the night was quiet.

Sometimes Chukwudi would ask her,

"Mama, why do you pray for me every day?"

Amara would smile.

"Because a mother's prayer does not become tired."

Chukwudi would laugh.

He loved hearing his mother's words.

But not every mother in Azabre spoke to her children like Amara.

There were women who became angry easily.

When their children made mistakes, they shouted at them.

Some even spoke words they did not think about.

One afternoon, Amara was returning from the farm when she heard a woman called Nneka speaking harshly to her young daughter, Obiageli.

"You never listen!"

"Why can't you do anything right?"

The little girl became quiet.

Amara stopped for a moment.

She did not judge Nneka.

She simply walked closer.

"Nneka, she is still learning."

Nneka looked at Amara.

"I know. Sometimes I just get tired."

Amara nodded.

"We all get tired."

"But our children remember the words we give them."

Nneka became quiet.

Amara placed her hand gently on Obiageli's shoulder.

"You are doing your best."

The little girl smiled.

Nneka looked at her daughter.

Her face softened.

"Come here, my child."

Obiageli moved closer to her mother.

Nneka hugged her.

"I am sorry."

Amara smiled and continued home.

That evening, Chukwudi returned from work.

Amara was waiting outside their small home.

She smiled when she saw him.

"Welcome home, my son."

"Thank you, Mama."

He sat beside her.

For a while, they watched the evening sky.

Then Chukwudi asked,

"Mama, did you pray for me today?"

Amara laughed softly.

"Of course."

"Even when I was not here?"

Amara nodded.

"Especially when you were not here."

Chukwudi rested his head on her shoulder.

Amara placed her hand on his head.

Then she prayed quietly.

"May you grow into a man who brings peace wherever he goes."

Chukwudi closed his eyes.

He listened.

He did not know it yet, but the simple prayers of his mother would become one of the greatest gifts he would carry through life.

And in Azabre, people would soon begin to understand that a child does not only need food for the body.

A child also needs good words for the heart.

🌙 End of Chapter One

🌅 Chapter Two – Words That Follow a Child

The days in Azabre moved quietly.

Every morning, Amara woke before Chukwudi.

She prepared his food.

She made sure his clothes were clean.

And before he stepped outside, she prayed.

"Go in peace, my son."

"Learn something good today."

"And remember to be kind."

Chukwudi always answered,

"Amen, Mama."

One morning, Chukwudi was late for work.

He had forgotten where he placed his sandals.

He searched around the house.

Amara did not shout.

She simply helped him look.

"Check beside the wooden stool."

Chukwudi looked.

There they were.

"I found them!"

Amara smiled.

"Good. Now you can go."

On the road, Chukwudi met his friend Emeka.

Emeka was carrying a bundle of cassava.

His mother had sent him to sell it.

The two boys walked together.

After a while, Emeka said,

"Your mother never gets angry with you."

Chukwudi laughed.

"She gets angry sometimes."

Emeka shook his head.

"My mother calls me useless when I make mistakes."

Chukwudi became quiet.

He did not know what to say.

Emeka continued walking.

"Sometimes I believe her."

Chukwudi looked at his friend.

"Don't."

Emeka smiled a little.

They continued toward the farms.

Later that afternoon, Chukwudi returned home.

He found his mother sitting outside.

He sat beside her.

"Mama."

"Yes, my son?"

"Do words really matter?"

Amara looked at him.

"Very much."

"Why?"

She smiled.

"Because a child can forget what you bought for him."

"But he may remember what you told him for the rest of his life."

Chukwudi thought about Emeka.

He thought about how his friend had spoken about his mother.

Amara continued.

"That is why I pray for you."

"I want you to know that you can make mistakes and still become a good man."

Chukwudi smiled.

"Thank you, Mama."

That evening, Amara visited her friend Adanna.

Adanna had three children.

Her youngest son, Kelechi, had broken a clay cup.

Adanna was upset.

"Kelechi, you are always breaking things!"

The boy lowered his head.

Amara walked closer.

"It was only a cup."

Adanna sighed.

"I know. I am just tired."

Amara nodded.

"Then rest before you speak."

Adanna looked at her.

For a moment, neither woman spoke.

Then Adanna called her son.

"Kelechi."

The boy looked up.

"I know you did not break it on purpose."

Kelechi nodded.

Adanna pulled him closer.

"Next time, be more careful."

"Yes, Mama."

Amara smiled.

She did not tell Adanna how to raise her children.

She only reminded her that tired hearts can speak words they do not mean.

That night, Amara returned home.

Chukwudi was already asleep.

She stood beside his sleeping mat.

She placed her hand gently on his head.

Then she whispered her usual prayer.

"May your heart remain soft."

"May your mind remain wise."

"May you never use your strength to make another person feel small."

She covered him with his cloth.

Then she sat quietly beside him.

Amara knew that raising a child was not only about feeding him.

It was also about the words spoken when nobody else was listening.

And every prayer she whispered was another seed she planted in her son's heart.

🌙 End of Chapter Two

🌙 Chapter Three – The Prayer Before Sunrise

One morning, Azabre was still quiet when Amara woke up.

She stepped outside and looked at the sky.

The first light of morning was beginning to appear.

Chukwudi was still sleeping.

Amara sat beside the doorway.

She folded her hands and began to pray.

"Thank you for another morning."

"Keep my son safe wherever he goes."

"Give him wisdom when he has a choice to make."

"Give him courage when things become difficult."

She prayed quietly.

She did not ask for riches.

She did not ask for a big house.

She only asked that her son should grow into a good man.

Chukwudi woke up and heard her voice.

He stayed on his mat for a while.

He listened.

When Amara finished, he came outside.

"Mama."

Amara turned.

"You are awake."

Chukwudi smiled.

"I heard you praying."

Amara smiled back.

"Then you have heard your mother's heart."

Chukwudi sat beside her.

"Do you pray for me because you are afraid something will happen to me?"

Amara shook her head.

"No."

"I pray because I want you to remember that you are loved."

Chukwudi looked at her.

He did not answer.

He simply rested his head on her shoulder.

Later that morning, Chukwudi went to help at the farm.

On his way, he met Emeka.

Emeka looked unhappy.

"What happened?"

Emeka shrugged.

"Nothing."

Chukwudi knew his friend was not telling the truth.

They walked together.

After some time, Emeka spoke.

"My mother said I will never become anything."

Chukwudi became quiet.

He remembered his mother's words from the night before.

"Don't believe that."

Emeka looked at him.

"How do you know?"

Chukwudi smiled.

"Because my mother always tells me that a mistake does not decide a person's whole life."

Emeka smiled.

"I wish my mother spoke like yours."

Chukwudi placed his hand on his friend's shoulder.

"Maybe she will learn."

That afternoon, something simple happened in Azabre.

A young girl named Amaka was carrying a heavy basket.

The basket slipped from her head.

Everything inside fell onto the ground.

Some people walked past.

Chukwudi stopped.

He helped her pick everything up.

Amaka smiled.

"Thank you."

Chukwudi smiled.

"My mother would ask me why I walked past you."

Amaka laughed.

That evening, Chukwudi told Amara what happened.

She smiled.

"You did well."

Chukwudi nodded.

"You taught me."

Amara shook her head.

"I only showed you what I believe."

She placed her hand on his head.

Then she prayed again.

This time, Chukwudi joined her.

"May we always help people when we can."

Amara smiled.

"Amen."

The two sat together as the evening settled over Azabre.

Amara had always believed that prayer was not only asking for protection.

It was also asking for the wisdom to become a better person.

And slowly, Chukwudi was learning the same thing.

He was beginning to understand that the greatest thing his mother gave him was not money.

It was not land.

It was not possessions.

It was the words she placed inside his heart every day.

Words that would stay with him wherever life took him.

🌙 End of Chapter Three

🌿 Chapter Four – The Lesson of the Mothers

As the days passed, the people of Azabre began to notice something about Chukwudi.

He was becoming more patient.

He listened when older people spoke.

He helped people without waiting to be asked.

And whenever he made a mistake, he did not give up.

Amara noticed all these things, but she never praised herself for them.

One afternoon, several women gathered near the village meeting place.

Among them were Nneka, Adanna, Ogechi, and Uchechi.

Their children were playing nearby.

Ogechi watched her young son, Somto, trying to carry a small basket.

The basket fell from his hands.

Ogechi became upset.

"Somto, you are always making mistakes!"

The boy looked down.

Before she could say more, Amara gently touched her arm.

"Let him try again."

Ogechi sighed.

"Sometimes I forget he is still a child."

Amara smiled.

"We all forget sometimes."

Somto picked up the basket again.

This time, he carried it successfully.

Ogechi smiled.

"Well done, my son."

Somto smiled back.

The women became quiet.

Adanna looked at Amara.

"How do you always know what to say?"

Amara laughed.

"I don't always know."

"I just remember how small our children are."

Nneka nodded.

"Sometimes we speak when we are angry."

Amara agreed.

"That happens."

"But when we calm down, we should remember that words cannot be collected back once they leave our mouths."

The women thought about it.

That evening, each woman went home thinking about her children.

Nneka sat beside Obiageli.

She held her daughter's hand.

"My child, I want you to know something."

Obiageli looked at her.

"You will make mistakes."

"But I will always want you to learn and become better."

Obiageli smiled.

Across the village, Adanna spoke gently to Kelechi.

Ogechi helped Somto prepare for the next day's work.

Little by little, the women began changing the way they spoke to their children.

Not because Amara forced them.

Not because anyone ordered them.

They simply began to understand.

A child needs correction.

But a child also needs encouragement.

Later that night, Chukwudi sat outside with his mother.

The village was quiet.

He looked at Amara.

"Mama, do you think your prayers are working?"

Amara smiled.

"What makes you ask?"

"People are speaking differently to their children now."

Amara looked toward the houses.

"That is a good thing."

Chukwudi smiled.

"Then your prayers are helping more than me."

Amara laughed.

"Prayer is not only for one person."

"When we pray for someone to become better, we should also become better ourselves."

Chukwudi nodded.

Amara placed her hand on his head.

She prayed once more.

"May you never forget kindness."

"May you respect those older than you."

"May you have patience with those younger than you."

"And may you always remember that every person deserves good words."

Chukwudi answered,

"Amen."

The following morning, something beautiful happened.

The children of Azabre gathered near the village square.

They began greeting the elders before going to play.

They helped carry small things.

They shared food.

They laughed together.

The mothers watched them.

Amara stood quietly among the women.

She did not say anything.

She only smiled.

For her, this was enough.

She had prayed for one child.

But her prayers had slowly touched many hearts.

And Azabre was becoming a warmer place, one family at a time.

🌙 End of Chapter Four

🌤️ Chapter Five – The Blessing of a Good Heart

The people of Azabre had started noticing a small change in the village.

Mothers were becoming gentler with their children.

Children were learning to listen.

The elders were being greeted with respect.

And Amara continued with her simple life.

Every morning, she prayed for Chukwudi.

She never knew that her quiet prayers had become an example to other mothers.

One morning, Chukwudi came home from the farm with a young boy named Obinna.

Obinna looked tired.

Amara gave him water.

"Welcome, my son."

Obinna looked surprised.

"My mother is angry with me."

Amara sat beside him.

"What happened?"

Obinna explained that he had damaged one of his mother's farming tools.

He was afraid to go home.

Amara smiled gently.

"Mistakes can be corrected."

"Go home and tell your mother what happened."

Obinna shook his head.

"She will shout at me."

Chukwudi spoke.

"Tell her the truth anyway."

Obinna finally nodded.

Before he left, Amara prayed for him.

"May you have courage to tell the truth."

Obinna smiled.

"Amen."

He returned home.

Later that evening, his mother came to Amara's house.

Her name was Chiamaka.

She looked worried.

"Amara, my son told me everything."

Amara smiled.

"That is good."

Chiamaka sat down.

"I wanted to shout at him."

"But I remembered what you told us."

Amara nodded.

"Children need to know when they are wrong."

"But they also need to know that being wrong does not make them bad."

Chiamaka smiled.

She thanked Amara and went home.

Days passed.

Then something happened that made the whole village happy.

The elders of Azabre decided to honour the mothers who had helped make the village more peaceful.

Nobody expected Amara to be called.

But when the village elder, Elder Nwafor, stood before everyone, he mentioned her name.

"Amara has never asked for praise."

"But her words have helped many children."

Amara became shy.

She looked at Chukwudi.

He was smiling proudly.

Elder Nwafor continued.

"A good mother does not only raise her own child."

"Her example can teach an entire village."

The women applauded.

Nneka hugged Amara.

Adanna smiled.

Ogechi wiped a tear from her eye.

Even the children were happy.

Chukwudi walked toward his mother.

"Mama."

Amara looked at him.

"I am proud of you."

Amara smiled.

"I am proud of you too."

That evening, when everyone had gone home, Chukwudi sat beside his mother.

The village was peaceful.

He remembered all the mornings when she had placed her hand on his head.

He remembered every prayer.

He finally understood that she had never prayed for him to become rich or famous.

She had prayed for his heart.

And that was the greatest gift she could give him.

Amara looked at him.

"Tomorrow, I will pray for you again."

Chukwudi smiled.

"And I will pray for you too, Mama."

Amara laughed softly.

She placed her hand on his head.

This time, they prayed together.

And somewhere in Azabre, another mother was doing the same with her child.

The simple prayer of one mother had become a lesson shared by many.

Not through force.

Not through fear.

But through patience, love, and good words.

🌙 End of Chapter Five
