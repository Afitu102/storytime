🕯 When Ancestors Call

🌍 African Folktales • 🕒 30 min read

⛓️ Chapter One – The Boy Who Heard His Name

Long after the last cooking fire had gone out, a boy named Temba woke to the sound of chains.

Not one chain.

Many chains.

They dragged across stone outside his room.

Slowly.

Then they stopped.

Temba sat up.

His little oil lamp was almost dead.

The room was dark.

He listened.

Nothing.

He pulled the blanket around his shoulders.

Then he heard it.

"Temba..."

His eyes opened wide.

Nobody called him by that name at night.

His mother always called him "my son."

His father called him "boy."

But the voice outside had said his name.

Temba climbed down from his sleeping mat.

He picked up the lamp.

The flame shook in his hand.

He walked to the door.

Before he could open it, his grandmother's voice came from the other side.

"Do not open that door."

Temba froze.

"Grandmother?"

There was no answer.

Temba moved closer.

"Grandmother, is that you?"

A long silence followed.

Then she spoke again.

"Go back to your mat."

Temba swallowed.

"But I heard someone."

"I know."

"Who was it?"

His grandmother did not answer.

Instead, she whispered:

"Someone who should have forgotten you."

Temba's skin became cold.

He stepped away from the door.

Then something scraped the wood from outside.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

Temba looked down.

A thin white thread was sliding beneath the door.

It moved like a small snake.

Temba stepped back.

The thread stopped.

Then it formed three letters on the floor.

RUN.

Temba dropped the lamp.

The flame went out.

Darkness filled the room.

He heard his grandmother shout.

"Temba, don't look behind you!"

He turned anyway.

There was nobody there.

But the wall behind him had changed.

A tall shadow stood across it.

The shadow had no body.

Only a head.

And two long hands.

The hands were chained.

Temba screamed.

The shadow lifted one finger.

It pointed toward the window.

Temba ran.

He pushed the window open.

Cold air rushed into the room.

Outside, the village was asleep.

The moon was bright.

But something was wrong.

There were no dogs barking.

No crickets.

No night birds.

Even the trees were still.

Temba climbed through the window.

His feet touched the ground.

Then he heard chains again.

This time they came from the forest.

He looked toward the dark trees.

A blue light moved between them.

Then another.

Then another.

Temba wanted to run back inside.

But his grandmother shouted from the window.

"Go to the old shrine!"

Temba looked up.

"What?"

"Go now!"

Her voice shook.

"And whatever you see there, do not answer when they call your name."

Temba ran.

He did not know why the shrine was safer.

He only knew his grandmother had never sounded so afraid.

He crossed the village path.

He passed the old mango tree.

He passed the dry well.

Then he entered the forest.

Branches scratched his arms.

Roots caught his feet.

He almost fell twice.

Behind him, the chains followed.

Closer.

Closer.

Then a voice came from the trees.

"Temba..."

He stopped.

It was his father's voice.

"Temba, come back."

Temba almost turned.

Then he remembered his grandmother's warning.

Do not answer.

He kept running.

The voice changed.

Now it sounded like his mother.

"My son..."

Temba squeezed his eyes shut.

He kept moving.

Then the voice became his own.

"Temba..."

He stopped again.

He looked around.

The forest was empty.

Then someone whispered right beside his ear.

"You were not supposed to hear us."

Temba ran faster.

The trees suddenly opened.

He reached the old shrine.

It stood between two giant stones.

Nobody visited it anymore.

The doorway was covered with vines.

Temba pushed them aside.

Inside was a large stone room.

He stepped in.

The chains stopped.

Temba breathed hard.

For a moment, everything was quiet.

Then the door closed behind him.

Temba turned.

He tried to open it.

It would not move.

He pulled harder.

Nothing.

Then he heard a low sound.

It came from the far end of the shrine.

A man was sitting there.

He was very old.

His body looked almost see-through.

Long white cloth covered him.

Heavy chains were wrapped around his arms.

More chains covered his legs.

Temba stepped back.

The old man looked at him.

"You came."

Temba said nothing.

The old man smiled.

"Good."

Temba shook his head.

"Who are you?"

The old man looked toward the ceiling.

"We have been waiting for your family for a very long time."

Temba moved closer.

"Who is we?"

The old man pointed behind him.

Temba slowly turned.

There were people standing in the shadows.

Men.

Women.

Children.

Old people.

Young people.

All covered in pale cloth.

All surrounded by chains.

Some were sitting.

Some were standing.

Some were staring at the floor.

But every one of them was looking at Temba.

He could not breathe.

"Who are they?"

The old man answered softly.

"Your ancestors."

Temba shook his head.

"No."

The old man pointed at one woman.

She stepped forward.

Temba recognized her face.

He had seen her face in an old family picture.

His great-grandmother.

He had been told she died before he was born.

The woman smiled.

"Your grandmother told you we were dead."

Temba whispered:

"She said you all passed away."

The woman shook her head.

"We did."

Temba stared at her.

"Then how are you here?"

Nobody answered.

The chains began to move.

Slowly.

One by one.

The spirits turned toward the same wall.

Temba looked at it.

There was a large stone door.

It had no handle.

No lock.

Only one mark.

A small handprint.

The old man stood.

His chains dragged across the floor.

"That door has been closed for seventy years."

Temba stared at the handprint.

"Why?"

The old man looked at him.

"Because your family made a promise."

Temba's heart beat faster.

"What promise?"

The old man stepped closer.

He lifted one chained hand.

"That no child from your blood would ever open it."

Temba looked at the stone door.

Then at his own hand.

The handprint on the door was exactly his size.

His fingers began to shake.

The old man whispered:

"And tonight, the door has chosen you."

A loud crack filled the shrine.

A line appeared across the stone door.

The chains around every ancestor pulled tight.

Some cried out.

Some covered their faces.

The old man looked terrified for the first time.

"Do not touch it."

Temba stepped back.

But his hand moved by itself.

His palm slowly lifted.

The spirits began whispering.

All of them.

At the same time.

"Temba..."

He tried to pull his hand away.

He could not.

His palm touched the stone.

The whole shrine shook.

The door opened.

And from the darkness behind it came the voice of a woman.

"At last."

Temba knew that voice.

It was his grandmother.

But his grandmother was still outside the shrine.

And the woman behind the door laughed.

"Now bring me the boy."

🌙 End of Chapter One

🕯️ Chapter Two – Woman Behind the Door

Temba stood frozen.

The stone door was open.

Darkness filled the doorway.

He could hear someone breathing inside.

Slow.

Deep.

Then the voice came again.

"Bring me the boy."

Temba stepped backward.

The ancestors moved away from the doorway.

Even the old man with the chains looked afraid.

"Do not go near her."

Temba looked at him.

"Who is she?"

The old man shook his head.

"Someone your family tried to forget."

A hand appeared inside the doorway.

It was a woman's hand.

Thin fingers.

Long nails.

A black thread was tied around the wrist.

Temba moved back.

The hand disappeared.

Then a woman stepped into the light.

She wore a long brown cloth.

Her hair covered half of her face.

She looked at Temba.

Then she smiled.

"You have your mother's eyes."

Temba's heart jumped.

"You know my mother?"

The woman laughed softly.

"I knew her before she knew herself."

Temba did not understand.

He looked at the old man.

The old man lowered his head.

The woman walked closer.

"Come here."

Temba did not move.

"No."

The woman's smile disappeared.

"Your grandmother told you to run to this place."

Temba's eyes widened.

"How do you know that?"

The woman pointed toward the entrance.

"Because she is standing outside."

Temba turned.

The shrine entrance was closed.

But behind the wooden door, he could hear his grandmother knocking.

"Temba!"

He ran toward the door.

The woman grabbed his arm.

Her fingers were cold.

"Don't open it."

Temba pulled away.

"That's my grandmother!"

The woman stared at him.

"Is it?"

Temba stopped.

His grandmother knocked again.

"Temba, open the door!"

The woman whispered:

"Ask her what she called you when you were five."

Temba frowned.

He moved toward the door.

"Grandma?"

"What did you call me when I was five?"

Silence.

Temba waited.

Then his grandmother answered.

"My little goat."

Temba smiled.

That was right.

His grandmother always called him that.

He reached for the door.

The woman suddenly grabbed his shoulder.

"Ask her what happened on the day you fell into the well."

Temba stopped.

His smile disappeared.

That memory was known only by his grandmother and mother.

He leaned toward the door.

"Grandma, what happened when I fell into the well?"

Silence.

The knocking stopped.

Then something scratched the door.

Slowly.

From top to bottom.

Temba stepped away.

The woman whispered:

"Now you know."

The old man nodded.

"That is not your grandmother."

Temba stared at the door.

The voice came again.

"Temba..."

But this time it was not his grandmother's voice.

It sounded like his mother.

Then his father.

Then his own voice.

Then the voices of many children.

All calling him.

The woman stepped away from him.

"She is looking for a way inside."

Temba looked at her.

"Who?"

The woman pointed at the stone doorway behind them.

The darkness inside had grown.

It was no longer just a room.

It looked like a deep forest.

Temba could see trees moving inside it.

He could hear birds.

He could smell wet soil.

Then he saw something hanging from one of the trees.

A small red shirt.

Temba recognized it.

He had worn that shirt when he was six.

"How did that get there?"

The woman answered:

"The door keeps what people leave behind."

Temba moved closer.

The old man shouted:

"Stay back!"

Temba stopped.

The shirt began to move.

Someone was wearing it.

A little boy stepped from behind the tree.

He looked exactly like Temba.

Same face.

Same eyes.

Same small scar above the eyebrow.

Temba could not speak.

The little boy walked toward the doorway.

He stopped.

He looked at Temba.

"You left me here."

Temba shook his head.

"I don't know you."

The boy smiled.

"You will."

The woman looked away.

The old man closed his eyes.

Temba felt a strange pain in his head.

Images rushed through his mind.

A well.

Rain.

His mother crying.

His grandmother shouting.

A little hand reaching from the darkness.

Then nothing.

Temba fell to his knees.

The woman caught him.

"Don't remember everything at once."

Temba looked at her.

"What happened to me?"

She did not answer.

Instead, she removed the black thread from her wrist.

Under it was a deep mark.

A handprint.

Temba looked at his own wrist.

There was the same mark.

He pulled down his sleeve.

"I've had this since I was little."

The woman nodded.

"That is why the ancestors called you."

Temba stood slowly.

"Called me for what?"

The old man answered.

"To finish what your family started."

The stone floor suddenly cracked.

A golden light came through the crack.

The ancestors began to move.

Their chains became loose.

One by one, they stood.

The woman stepped aside.

The old man pointed toward the floor.

"Look."

A small wooden box rose from beneath the stone.

It was covered in dust.

Temba walked toward it.

There was a name carved into the lid.

AMAECHI.

The old man whispered:

"That was your great-grandfather."

Temba opened the box.

Inside was an old piece of cloth.

It was covered in names.

Hundreds of them.

But one name had been scratched away.

Temba touched the empty space.

The cloth became warm.

Letters slowly appeared.

TEMBA.

Temba stepped back.

"My name was here?"

The old man shook his head.

"Not your name."

Temba looked at him.

"Then whose?"

The old man pointed at the little boy standing inside the dark forest.

The boy smiled.

"Mine."

Temba's heart stopped for a moment.

The woman grabbed his hand.

"We have to leave."

Temba looked at the door.

The fake voice had gone quiet.

He looked at the little boy.

The boy was still smiling.

"Don't leave me again."

Temba turned away.

The woman pulled him toward a small opening behind the shrine wall.

They squeezed through.

The old man followed.

The ancestors remained behind.

Temba looked back one last time.

The little boy was no longer there.

Only the red shirt remained.

Then the stone door slammed shut.

Temba and the woman fell onto wet grass outside.

The sun was beginning to rise.

Temba looked around.

The village was still quiet.

Then he saw his grandmother.

She was sitting beneath a tree.

Her hands were tied.

She was alive.

Temba rushed toward her.

"Grandma!"

She looked up.

Tears filled her eyes.

"You opened it."

Temba nodded.

"Who was the boy?"

Grandma looked at the woman beside him.

Her face changed.

"You found her."

Temba looked between them.

"Who is she?"

Grandma whispered:

"Your aunt."

The woman looked down.

Temba stared at her.

"I have an aunt?"

His grandmother nodded.

"Yes."

Then she looked toward the forest.

"And she was supposed to die twenty years ago."

Temba's blood ran cold.

The woman slowly turned toward the forest.

Something moved between the trees.

A little boy in a red shirt.

He lifted his hand.

He waved at Temba.

Then he disappeared.

Grandma began to cry.

"Now he knows you remember him."

🌙 End of Chapter Two

🌑 Chapter Three – Name Buried in the Ground

Nobody spoke as they walked back toward the village.

Grandma walked in front.

Temba stayed beside the woman.

He still could not believe what Grandma had said.

His aunt.

A woman he had never heard about.

A woman who was supposed to have died twenty years ago.

The woman kept her eyes on the ground.

Temba finally broke the silence.

"What is your name?"

She stopped.

Grandma stopped too.

The woman looked at Temba.

"Chinwe."

Grandma turned sharply.

"Don't say that name here."

Temba looked around.

They were still at the edge of the forest.

There was nobody nearby.

"Why?"

Grandma lowered her voice.

"Because the ground remembers names."

Temba frowned.

Chinwe gave a tired smile.

"She still believes that."

Grandma looked at her.

"I don't believe it."

"I have seen it."

They continued walking.

Soon the first houses appeared.

Smoke rose from cooking fires.

Women were sweeping their yards.

Children were carrying buckets.

The village was waking.

But when people saw Chinwe beside Grandma, they stopped.

One woman dropped the broom in her hand.

An old man stepped out of his doorway.

Nobody said a word.

Chinwe kept walking.

Temba looked from face to face.

"Why are they looking at us like that?"

Grandma did not answer.

They reached her house.

Grandma opened the door.

Inside, everything looked normal.

A wooden table.

Three stools.

A clay pot beside the wall.

Old family pictures above the bed.

Temba looked at the pictures.

He had seen them many times.

But now something looked different.

There was an empty space between two pictures.

A small nail was sticking out of the wall.

"Grandma."

She turned.

"Where is that picture?"

Grandma looked at the empty space.

Her face became hard.

"Which picture?"

Temba pointed.

"There was always another one there."

Chinwe walked closer.

She stared at the empty nail.

"You kept it."

Grandma shook her head.

"I destroyed it."

Chinwe looked at her.

"No."

"You hid it."

Grandma said nothing.

Temba felt something cold move through the room.

"What picture?"

Chinwe pointed at the wall.

"A picture of the three of us."

Temba stared at her.

"Three?"

Chinwe nodded.

"Me."

"Your mother."

"And the boy."

Temba's stomach tightened.

"The boy in the red shirt?"

Chinwe nodded.

Grandma suddenly slammed the door.

"Enough."

Temba jumped.

Grandma walked to the kitchen.

She opened a wooden box.

Inside were old clothes.

She pushed them aside.

Underneath was a small leather bag.

She carried it to the table.

Then she opened it.

Inside was a small bundle of red thread.

Temba stared at it.

The thread moved.

Just once.

Like something had pulled it.

Grandma quickly closed the bag.

"Nobody touches this."

Temba looked at Chinwe.

Chinwe was staring at the bag.

"That is what he wants."

Grandma looked at her.

"He already has enough."

"Not anymore."

Temba leaned forward.

"Who are you talking about?"

Neither woman answered.

Then something knocked on the window.

Once.

Temba turned.

Nothing was there.

Another knock.

This time from the other window.

Then the door.

Three knocks.

Temba stood.

"Who is outside?"

Grandma grabbed his arm.

"Sit down."

The knocking stopped.

A small voice came from outside.

"Grandma."

Temba's heart jumped.

It was the little boy.

"Grandma, open the door."

Chinwe covered her mouth.

Grandma closed her eyes.

"Don't answer."

The boy knocked again.

"I am hungry."

Temba looked at Grandma.

"Maybe he needs help."

Grandma shook her head.

"That boy has not been hungry for twenty years."

The voice outside became quiet.

Then it laughed.

Not like a child.

Like an old man.

The windows began to shake.

The red thread inside the leather bag started moving faster.

Grandma opened the bag.

She pulled out the thread.

A small needle was tied to it.

She held it tightly.

"Stay behind me."

Temba and Chinwe moved back.

The front door began to bend.

Something was pushing it from outside.

Grandma raised the needle.

She whispered words Temba had never heard before.

The door stopped moving.

The room became quiet.

Then the red thread pulled itself from Grandma's hand.

It flew toward Temba.

It wrapped around his wrist.

Temba cried out.

The thread tightened.

A red mark appeared on his skin.

Chinwe rushed forward.

"Cut it!"

Grandma grabbed the scissors.

She tried to cut the thread.

The scissors would not close.

The thread pulled Temba toward the door.

He fought back.

His feet slid across the floor.

"Grandma!"

Grandma grabbed his waist.

Chinwe grabbed Grandma.

All three pulled.

The thread became tighter.

Then the door opened by itself.

Nobody stood outside.

But muddy footprints appeared on the ground.

One after another.

They entered the house.

Small footprints.

They stopped in front of Temba.

The thread suddenly went loose.

Temba fell backward.

The footprints turned toward the wall.

They walked straight up it.

Then disappeared.

Everyone stood still.

Temba looked at Grandma.

"What does he want?"

Grandma looked at the red thread.

"He wants you to remember."

Temba shook his head.

"Remember what?"

Grandma walked toward the empty space on the wall.

She touched the nail.

"The night your mother gave birth."

Chinwe looked down.

Temba stared at her.

"What happened that night?"

Grandma took a deep breath.

"You were not the only child born that night."

Temba felt the room spin.

"What?"

Grandma pointed toward the floor.

"Your mother had twins."

Temba looked at Chinwe.

She was crying.

"One of you was taken."

Temba could barely speak.

"Taken by who?"

Grandma looked toward the forest.

"Your ancestors."

A loud bell rang outside.

Once.

Twice.

Then a third time.

The red thread moved across the floor.

It formed a shape.

A small child's hand.

Then one word appeared beneath it.

COME.

Temba stared at the message.

Chinwe whispered:

"He has found us."

Grandma picked up the scissors.

"No."

"This time, we find him."

Outside, something small ran past the window.

Temba looked.

A red shirt disappeared behind the house.

Then came a child's laughter.

Very close.

"Temba..."

He turned toward the sound.

A small handprint appeared on the inside of the bedroom door.

Then another.

Then another.

The prints slowly climbed upward.

And stopped at the ceiling.

Temba looked up.

Two small eyes were staring down at him.

And the boy whispered:

"Brother."
