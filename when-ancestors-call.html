⬅ Back to African Folktales

🕯 When Ancestors Call

🌍 African Folktales • 🕒 30 min read

⛓️ Chapter One – The Boy Who Heard His Name

Long after the last cooking fire had gone out, a boy named Temba woke to the sound of chains.

Not one chain.

Many chains.

They dragged across stone outside his room.

Slowly.

Then they stopped.

Temba sat up.

His little oil lamp was almost dead.

The room was dark.

He listened.

Nothing.

He pulled the blanket around his shoulders.

Then he heard it.

"Temba..."

His eyes opened wide.

Nobody called him by that name at night.

His mother always called him "my son."

His father called him "boy."

But the voice outside had said his name.

Temba climbed down from his sleeping mat.

He picked up the lamp.

The flame shook in his hand.

He walked to the door.

Before he could open it, his grandmother's voice came from the other side.

"Do not open that door."

Temba froze.

"Grandmother?"

There was no answer.

Temba moved closer.

"Grandmother, is that you?"

A long silence followed.

Then she spoke again.

"Go back to your mat."

Temba swallowed.

"But I heard someone."

"I know."

"Who was it?"

His grandmother did not answer.

Instead, she whispered:

"Someone who should have forgotten you."

Temba's skin became cold.

He stepped away from the door.

Then something scraped the wood from outside.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

Temba looked down.

A thin white thread was sliding beneath the door.

It moved like a small snake.

Temba stepped back.

The thread stopped.

Then it formed three letters on the floor.

RUN.

Temba dropped the lamp.

The flame went out.

Darkness filled the room.

He heard his grandmother shout.

"Temba, don't look behind you!"

He turned anyway.

There was nobody there.

But the wall behind him had changed.

A tall shadow stood across it.

The shadow had no body.

Only a head.

And two long hands.

The hands were chained.

Temba screamed.

The shadow lifted one finger.

It pointed toward the window.

Temba ran.

He pushed the window open.

Cold air rushed into the room.

Outside, the village was asleep.

The moon was bright.

But something was wrong.

There were no dogs barking.

No crickets.

No night birds.

Even the trees were still.

Temba climbed through the window.

His feet touched the ground.

Then he heard chains again.

This time they came from the forest.

He looked toward the dark trees.

A blue light moved between them.

Then another.

Then another.

Temba wanted to run back inside.

But his grandmother shouted from the window.

"Go to the old shrine!"

Temba looked up.

"What?"

"Go now!"

Her voice shook.

"And whatever you see there, do not answer when they call your name."

Temba ran.

He did not know why the shrine was safer.

He only knew his grandmother had never sounded so afraid.

He crossed the village path.

He passed the old mango tree.

He passed the dry well.

Then he entered the forest.

Branches scratched his arms.

Roots caught his feet.

He almost fell twice.

Behind him, the chains followed.

Closer.

Closer.

Then a voice came from the trees.

"Temba..."

He stopped.

It was his father's voice.

"Temba, come back."

Temba almost turned.

Then he remembered his grandmother's warning.

Do not answer.

He kept running.

The voice changed.

Now it sounded like his mother.

"My son..."

Temba squeezed his eyes shut.

He kept moving.

Then the voice became his own.

"Temba..."

He stopped again.

He looked around.

The forest was empty.

Then someone whispered right beside his ear.

"You were not supposed to hear us."

Temba ran faster.

The trees suddenly opened.

He reached the old shrine.

It stood between two giant stones.

Nobody visited it anymore.

The doorway was covered with vines.

Temba pushed them aside.

Inside was a large stone room.

He stepped in.

The chains stopped.

Temba breathed hard.

For a moment, everything was quiet.

Then the door closed behind him.

Temba turned.

He tried to open it.

It would not move.

He pulled harder.

Nothing.

Then he heard a low sound.

It came from the far end of the shrine.

A man was sitting there.

He was very old.

His body looked almost see-through.

Long white cloth covered him.

Heavy chains were wrapped around his arms.

More chains covered his legs.

Temba stepped back.

The old man looked at him.

"You came."

Temba said nothing.

The old man smiled.

"Good."

Temba shook his head.

"Who are you?"

The old man looked toward the ceiling.

"We have been waiting for your family for a very long time."

Temba moved closer.

"Who is we?"

The old man pointed behind him.

Temba slowly turned.

There were people standing in the shadows.

Men.

Women.

Children.

Old people.

Young people.

All covered in pale cloth.

All surrounded by chains.

Some were sitting.

Some were standing.

Some were staring at the floor.

But every one of them was looking at Temba.

He could not breathe.

"Who are they?"

The old man answered softly.

"Your ancestors."

Temba shook his head.

"No."

The old man pointed at one woman.

She stepped forward.

Temba recognized her face.

He had seen her face in an old family picture.

His great-grandmother.

He had been told she died before he was born.

The woman smiled.

"Your grandmother told you we were dead."

Temba whispered:

"She said you all passed away."

The woman shook her head.

"We did."

Temba stared at her.

"Then how are you here?"

Nobody answered.

The chains began to move.

Slowly.

One by one.

The spirits turned toward the same wall.

Temba looked at it.

There was a large stone door.

It had no handle.

No lock.

Only one mark.

A small handprint.

The old man stood.

His chains dragged across the floor.

"That door has been closed for seventy years."

Temba stared at the handprint.

"Why?"

The old man looked at him.

"Because your family made a promise."

Temba's heart beat faster.

"What promise?"

The old man stepped closer.

He lifted one chained hand.

"That no child from your blood would ever open it."

Temba looked at the stone door.

Then at his own hand.

The handprint on the door was exactly his size.

His fingers began to shake.

The old man whispered:

"And tonight, the door has chosen you."

A loud crack filled the shrine.

A line appeared across the stone door.

The chains around every ancestor pulled tight.

Some cried out.

Some covered their faces.

The old man looked terrified for the first time.

"Do not touch it."

Temba stepped back.

But his hand moved by itself.

His palm slowly lifted.

The spirits began whispering.

All of them.

At the same time.

"Temba..."

He tried to pull his hand away.

He could not.

His palm touched the stone.

The whole shrine shook.

The door opened.

And from the darkness behind it came the voice of a woman.

"At last."

Temba knew that voice.

It was his grandmother.

But his grandmother was still outside the shrine.

And the woman behind the door laughed.

"Now bring me the boy."

🌙 End of Chapter One

🕯️ Chapter Two – Woman Behind the Door

Temba stood frozen.

The stone door was open.

Darkness filled the doorway.

He could hear someone breathing inside.

Slow.

Deep.

Then the voice came again.

"Bring me the boy."

Temba stepped backward.

The ancestors moved away from the doorway.

Even the old man with the chains looked afraid.

"Do not go near her."

Temba looked at him.

"Who is she?"

The old man shook his head.

"Someone your family tried to forget."

A hand appeared inside the doorway.

It was a woman's hand.

Thin fingers.

Long nails.

A black thread was tied around the wrist.

Temba moved back.

The hand disappeared.

Then a woman stepped into the light.

She wore a long brown cloth.

Her hair covered half of her face.

She looked at Temba.

Then she smiled.

"You have your mother's eyes."

Temba's heart jumped.

"You know my mother?"

The woman laughed softly.

"I knew her before she knew herself."

Temba did not understand.

He looked at the old man.

The old man lowered his head.

The woman walked closer.

"Come here."

Temba did not move.

"No."

The woman's smile disappeared.

"Your grandmother told you to run to this place."

Temba's eyes widened.

"How do you know that?"

The woman pointed toward the entrance.

"Because she is standing outside."

Temba turned.

The shrine entrance was closed.

But behind the wooden door, he could hear his grandmother knocking.

"Temba!"

He ran toward the door.

The woman grabbed his arm.

Her fingers were cold.

"Don't open it."

Temba pulled away.

"That's my grandmother!"

The woman stared at him.

"Is it?"

Temba stopped.

His grandmother knocked again.

"Temba, open the door!"

The woman whispered:

"Ask her what she called you when you were five."

Temba frowned.

He moved toward the door.

"Grandma?"

"What did you call me when I was five?"

Silence.

Temba waited.

Then his grandmother answered.

"My little goat."

Temba smiled.

That was right.

His grandmother always called him that.

He reached for the door.

The woman suddenly grabbed his shoulder.

"Ask her what happened on the day you fell into the well."

Temba stopped.

His smile disappeared.

That memory was known only by his grandmother and mother.

He leaned toward the door.

"Grandma, what happened when I fell into the well?"

Silence.

The knocking stopped.

Then something scratched the door.

Slowly.

From top to bottom.

Temba stepped away.

The woman whispered:

"Now you know."

The old man nodded.

"That is not your grandmother."

Temba stared at the door.

The voice came again.

"Temba..."

But this time it was not his grandmother's voice.

It sounded like his mother.

Then his father.

Then his own voice.

Then the voices of many children.

All calling him.

The woman stepped away from him.

"She is looking for a way inside."

Temba looked at her.

"Who?"

The woman pointed at the stone doorway behind them.

The darkness inside had grown.

It was no longer just a room.

It looked like a deep forest.

Temba could see trees moving inside it.

He could hear birds.

He could smell wet soil.

Then he saw something hanging from one of the trees.

A small red shirt.

Temba recognized it.

He had worn that shirt when he was six.

"How did that get there?"

The woman answered:

"The door keeps what people leave behind."

Temba moved closer.

The old man shouted:

"Stay back!"

Temba stopped.

The shirt began to move.

Someone was wearing it.

A little boy stepped from behind the tree.

He looked exactly like Temba.

Same face.

Same eyes.

Same small scar above the eyebrow.

Temba could not speak.

The little boy walked toward the doorway.

He stopped.

He looked at Temba.

"You left me here."

Temba shook his head.

"I don't know you."

The boy smiled.

"You will."

The woman looked away.

The old man closed his eyes.

Temba felt a strange pain in his head.

Images rushed through his mind.

A well.

Rain.

His mother crying.

His grandmother shouting.

A little hand reaching from the darkness.

Then nothing.

Temba fell to his knees.

The woman caught him.

"Don't remember everything at once."

Temba looked at her.

"What happened to me?"

She did not answer.

Instead, she removed the black thread from her wrist.

Under it was a deep mark.

A handprint.

Temba looked at his own wrist.

There was the same mark.

He pulled down his sleeve.

"I've had this since I was little."

The woman nodded.

"That is why the ancestors called you."

Temba stood slowly.

"Called me for what?"

The old man answered.

"To finish what your family started."

The stone floor suddenly cracked.

A golden light came through the crack.

The ancestors began to move.

Their chains became loose.

One by one, they stood.

The woman stepped aside.

The old man pointed toward the floor.

"Look."

A small wooden box rose from beneath the stone.

It was covered in dust.

Temba walked toward it.

There was a name carved into the lid.

AMAECHI.

The old man whispered:

"That was your great-grandfather."

Temba opened the box.

Inside was an old piece of cloth.

It was covered in names.

Hundreds of them.

But one name had been scratched away.

Temba touched the empty space.

The cloth became warm.

Letters slowly appeared.

TEMBA.

Temba stepped back.

"My name was here?"

The old man shook his head.

"Not your name."

Temba looked at him.

"Then whose?"

The old man pointed at the little boy standing inside the dark forest.

The boy smiled.

"Mine."

Temba's heart stopped for a moment.

The woman grabbed his hand.

"We have to leave."

Temba looked at the door.

The fake voice had gone quiet.

He looked at the little boy.

The boy was still smiling.

"Don't leave me again."

Temba turned away.

The woman pulled him toward a small opening behind the shrine wall.

They squeezed through.

The old man followed.

The ancestors remained behind.

Temba looked back one last time.

The little boy was no longer there.

Only the red shirt remained.

Then the stone door slammed shut.

Temba and the woman fell onto wet grass outside.

The sun was beginning to rise.

Temba looked around.

The village was still quiet.

Then he saw his grandmother.

She was sitting beneath a tree.

Her hands were tied.

She was alive.

Temba rushed toward her.

"Grandma!"

She looked up.

Tears filled her eyes.

"You opened it."

Temba nodded.

"Who was the boy?"

Grandma looked at the woman beside him.

Her face changed.

"You found her."

Temba looked between them.

"Who is she?"

Grandma whispered:

"Your aunt."

The woman looked down.

Temba stared at her.

"I have an aunt?"

His grandmother nodded.

"Yes."

Then she looked toward the forest.

"And she was supposed to die twenty years ago."

Temba's blood ran cold.

The woman slowly turned toward the forest.

Something moved between the trees.

A little boy in a red shirt.

He lifted his hand.

He waved at Temba.

Then he disappeared.

Grandma began to cry.

"Now he knows you remember him."

🌙 End of Chapter Two

🌑 Chapter Three – Name Buried in the Ground

Nobody spoke as they walked back toward the village.

Grandma walked in front.

Temba stayed beside the woman.

He still could not believe what Grandma had said.

His aunt.

A woman he had never heard about.

A woman who was supposed to have died twenty years ago.

The woman kept her eyes on the ground.

Temba finally broke the silence.

"What is your name?"

She stopped.

Grandma stopped too.

The woman looked at Temba.

"Chinwe."

Grandma turned sharply.

"Don't say that name here."

Temba looked around.

They were still at the edge of the forest.

There was nobody nearby.

"Why?"

Grandma lowered her voice.

"Because the ground remembers names."

Temba frowned.

Chinwe gave a tired smile.

"She still believes that."

Grandma looked at her.

"I don't believe it."

"I have seen it."

They continued walking.

Soon the first houses appeared.

Smoke rose from cooking fires.

Women were sweeping their yards.

Children were carrying buckets.

The village was waking.

But when people saw Chinwe beside Grandma, they stopped.

One woman dropped the broom in her hand.

An old man stepped out of his doorway.

Nobody said a word.

Chinwe kept walking.

Temba looked from face to face.

"Why are they looking at us like that?"

Grandma did not answer.

They reached her house.

Grandma opened the door.

Inside, everything looked normal.

A wooden table.

Three stools.

A clay pot beside the wall.

Old family pictures above the bed.

Temba looked at the pictures.

He had seen them many times.

But now something looked different.

There was an empty space between two pictures.

A small nail was sticking out of the wall.

"Grandma."

She turned.

"Where is that picture?"

Grandma looked at the empty space.

Her face became hard.

"Which picture?"

Temba pointed.

"There was always another one there."

Chinwe walked closer.

She stared at the empty nail.

"You kept it."

Grandma shook her head.

"I destroyed it."

Chinwe looked at her.

"No."

"You hid it."

Grandma said nothing.

Temba felt something cold move through the room.

"What picture?"

Chinwe pointed at the wall.

"A picture of the three of us."

Temba stared at her.

"Three?"

Chinwe nodded.

"Me."

"Your mother."

"And the boy."

Temba's stomach tightened.

"The boy in the red shirt?"

Chinwe nodded.

Grandma suddenly slammed the door.

"Enough."

Temba jumped.

Grandma walked to the kitchen.

She opened a wooden box.

Inside were old clothes.

She pushed them aside.

Underneath was a small leather bag.

She carried it to the table.

Then she opened it.

Inside was a small bundle of red thread.

Temba stared at it.

The thread moved.

Just once.

Like something had pulled it.

Grandma quickly closed the bag.

"Nobody touches this."

Temba looked at Chinwe.

Chinwe was staring at the bag.

"That is what he wants."

Grandma looked at her.

"He already has enough."

"Not anymore."

Temba leaned forward.

"Who are you talking about?"

Neither woman answered.

Then something knocked on the window.

Once.

Temba turned.

Nothing was there.

Another knock.

This time from the other window.

Then the door.

Three knocks.

Temba stood.

"Who is outside?"

Grandma grabbed his arm.

"Sit down."

The knocking stopped.

A small voice came from outside.

"Grandma."

Temba's heart jumped.

It was the little boy.

"Grandma, open the door."

Chinwe covered her mouth.

Grandma closed her eyes.

"Don't answer."

The boy knocked again.

"I am hungry."

Temba looked at Grandma.

"Maybe he needs help."

Grandma shook her head.

"That boy has not been hungry for twenty years."

The voice outside became quiet.

Then it laughed.

Not like a child.

Like an old man.

The windows began to shake.

The red thread inside the leather bag started moving faster.

Grandma opened the bag.

She pulled out the thread.

A small needle was tied to it.

She held it tightly.

"Stay behind me."

Temba and Chinwe moved back.

The front door began to bend.

Something was pushing it from outside.

Grandma raised the needle.

She whispered words Temba had never heard before.

The door stopped moving.

The room became quiet.

Then the red thread pulled itself from Grandma's hand.

It flew toward Temba.

It wrapped around his wrist.

Temba cried out.

The thread tightened.

A red mark appeared on his skin.

Chinwe rushed forward.

"Cut it!"

Grandma grabbed the scissors.

She tried to cut the thread.

The scissors would not close.

The thread pulled Temba toward the door.

He fought back.

His feet slid across the floor.

"Grandma!"

Grandma grabbed his waist.

Chinwe grabbed Grandma.

All three pulled.

The thread became tighter.

Then the door opened by itself.

Nobody stood outside.

But muddy footprints appeared on the ground.

One after another.

They entered the house.

Small footprints.

They stopped in front of Temba.

The thread suddenly went loose.

Temba fell backward.

The footprints turned toward the wall.

They walked straight up it.

Then disappeared.

Everyone stood still.

Temba looked at Grandma.

"What does he want?"

Grandma looked at the red thread.

"He wants you to remember."

Temba shook his head.

"Remember what?"

Grandma walked toward the empty space on the wall.

She touched the nail.

"The night your mother gave birth."

Chinwe looked down.

Temba stared at her.

"What happened that night?"

Grandma took a deep breath.

"You were not the only child born that night."

Temba felt the room spin.

"What?"

Grandma pointed toward the floor.

"Your mother had twins."

Temba looked at Chinwe.

She was crying.

"One of you was taken."

Temba could barely speak.

"Taken by who?"

Grandma looked toward the forest.

"Your ancestors."

A loud bell rang outside.

Once.

Twice.

Then a third time.

The red thread moved across the floor.

It formed a shape.

A small child's hand.

Then one word appeared beneath it.

COME.

Temba stared at the message.

Chinwe whispered:

"He has found us."

Grandma picked up the scissors.

"No."

"This time, we find him."

Outside, something small ran past the window.

Temba looked.

A red shirt disappeared behind the house.

Then came a child's laughter.

Very close.

"Temba..."

He turned toward the sound.

A small handprint appeared on the inside of the bedroom door.

Then another.

Then another.

The prints slowly climbed upward.

And stopped at the ceiling.

Temba looked up.

Two small eyes were staring down at him.

And the boy whispered:

"Brother."

🌙 End of Chapter Three

🌒 Chapter Four – Brother in the Ceiling

Temba could not move.

Two small eyes stared down at him from the ceiling.

The face was upside down.

The same face he had seen in the shrine.

The same face that had followed him from the forest.

His brother.

Temba wanted to shout.

No sound came out.

Grandma grabbed his shoulder.

"Don't look at him."

But Temba could not look away.

The boy smiled.

"You remember me now."

Temba shook his head.

"I don't."

The boy's smile disappeared.

A tear rolled down his upside-down face.

"Then why do I remember you?"

The room became cold.

Chinwe stepped closer to Temba.

"Don't listen to him."

The boy looked at her.

"You left me too."

Chinwe closed her eyes.

Temba looked at her.

"You knew him?"

She did not answer.

Grandma raised the scissors.

"Enough."

She cut the red thread lying across the floor.

The moment the scissors touched it, the boy screamed.

The ceiling shook.

Dust fell everywhere.

Temba covered his face.

When he looked again, the boy was gone.

Only two wet footprints remained on the ceiling.

Then they slowly faded.

Temba turned toward Grandma.

"Tell me everything."

Grandma sat down.

For the first time, she looked old.

Very old.

She placed the scissors on the table.

"Your mother gave birth to two boys."

Temba listened.

Chinwe stood beside the window.

She looked like she already knew every word.

"You were born first."

"Your brother came a few minutes later."

Temba swallowed.

"What was his name?"

Grandma looked down.

"We called him Ikenna."

Temba repeated the name softly.

"Ikenna."

The red thread on the table moved.

Grandma quickly placed her hand over it.

"Don't say his name again."

Temba pulled back.

"Why?"

Grandma stared at him.

"Because names can open doors."

Temba remembered the shrine.

The stone door.

The voices.

The chains.

His brother.

He looked at Chinwe.

"What happened to Ikenna?"

Chinwe turned around.

"He was taken."

"By the ancestors?"

She nodded.

"Why?"

Chinwe looked at Grandma.

Grandma shook her head.

"Not yet."

Temba stood.

"I am tired of hearing that."

"Not yet."

"Then when?"

Grandma looked at him for a long moment.

"When the sun reaches the old hill."

Temba frowned.

"What happens then?"

Grandma looked toward the door.

"Your brother comes home."

Nobody spoke.

Temba's heart beat faster.

"That sounds good."

Grandma's face changed.

"No."

Temba stared at her.

"Why not?"

Grandma picked up the red thread.

"Because he cannot come home alone."

A loud sound came from outside.

Boom.

The ground shook.

Everyone rushed toward the window.

Far beyond the village, the old hill had begun to glow.

A pale light climbed slowly toward the top.

Grandma's face went white.

"We are out of time."

Temba looked at the hill.

"What is coming with him?"

Grandma did not answer.

Instead, Chinwe whispered:

"Everyone who was buried with him."

Temba turned.

"What?"

Chinwe's eyes filled with fear.

"Ikenna was not the only one taken."

Temba stepped back.

The room seemed smaller.

"How many?"

Chinwe looked toward the hill.

"Thirty-seven."

Temba stared at her.

"Thirty-seven people?"

She nodded.

"Thirty-seven children."

Grandma closed the curtains.

"Nobody leaves this house."

Temba moved toward the door.

"I need to find him."

Grandma blocked him.

"You don't understand."

"He called me his brother."

"And that is exactly why he wants you."

Temba stopped.

Grandma reached for the leather bag.

She opened it.

Inside were old pieces of cloth.

Each one had a name.

She placed them on the table.

One.

Two.

Three.

Temba counted them.

There were thirty-seven.

Every piece was dark around the edges.

Then Grandma placed one final piece on the table.

It was different.

White.

Clean.

No name was written on it.

Temba touched it.

The cloth became warm.

Words slowly appeared.

ONE MUST RETURN.

Temba looked at Grandma.

"What does that mean?"

Grandma whispered:

"It means only one of you can come back."

Temba's stomach tightened.

"Me or Ikenna?"

Grandma did not answer.

The old hill flashed with light.

Then the whole village went dark.

No lamps.

No moonlight.

Nothing.

A moment later, children began laughing outside.

One child.

Then another.

Then many.

Temba moved toward the window.

Grandma grabbed him.

"Don't look."

The children outside began singing.

A soft old song.

Temba knew the song.

His mother used to sing it when he was small.

But there was something different about it.

The children had changed the last line.

They sang:

"One brother sleeps, one brother wakes..."

Temba's body became cold.

The singing stopped.

Then someone knocked on the door.

Three times.

Grandma whispered:

"Don't move."

Another knock.

This time, a child's voice came through.

"Brother?"

Temba closed his eyes.

Ikenna.

His brother was outside.

"Brother, open the door."

Temba's eyes filled with tears.

He wanted to answer.

He wanted to see him.

But Grandma held his arm.

"Don't."

The voice became softer.

"I only want to come home."

Temba whispered:

"Why can't you come in?"

Silence.

Then the door slowly opened.

A small boy stood outside.

Red shirt.

Bare feet.

Wet hair.

He looked exactly like Temba.

But something was wrong.

His shadow was facing the wrong direction.

The boy smiled.

"Now you can see me."

Temba took one step forward.

Grandma shouted:

"NO!"

The boy's smile vanished.

His eyes became completely black.

The thirty-seven cloth pieces on the table lifted into the air.

They flew toward the door.

Each piece wrapped around the boy.

He screamed.

The ground outside cracked.

The old hill flashed again.

And behind Ikenna stood dozens of children.

All holding hands.

All staring at Temba.

Ikenna raised his hand.

"Come with us."

Then one of the children stepped forward.

It was a girl.

She looked at Temba and spoke in a voice that was not hers.

"Your mother knows why we were taken."

Temba stared at her.

His mother had been dead for years.

Then the girl smiled.

"And she is waiting for you at the hill."

The door slammed shut.

Temba turned toward Grandma.

Grandma was crying.

"Your mother is not dead."

🌙 End of Chapter Four

🌘 Chapter Five – Mother at the Old Hill

Temba stared at Grandma.

His mouth was open, but no words came out.

His mother was not dead.

For years, he had believed she was gone.

He remembered the day everyone told him she had left this world.

He remembered the crying.

He remembered the black clothes.

He remembered the long walk to the burial ground.

But now Grandma was saying something that made all those memories feel like a lie.

"Where is my mother?"

Grandma looked away.

"At the old hill."

Temba took a step toward her.

"Why did you tell me she was dead?"

Grandma's eyes filled with tears.

"Because I wanted you to live."

Temba shook his head.

"Live from what?"

Grandma looked at the closed door.

"From your own family."

A cold silence filled the room.

Chinwe sat on the floor.

She had not spoken since Grandma revealed the truth.

Temba turned to her.

"You knew my mother was alive."

Chinwe nodded.

"Yes."

"And you knew about Ikenna."

She nodded again.

Temba's voice became hard.

"Everybody knew except me."

Nobody answered.

Temba walked outside.

The village was strangely quiet.

People stood in their doorways.

Nobody looked directly at him.

He looked toward the old hill.

The pale light was still there.

It pulsed slowly.

Like a heartbeat.

Temba knew he had to go.

He turned back.

"I'm going to find her."

Grandma came outside.

"You cannot go alone."

Temba looked at Chinwe.

"Then come with me."

Chinwe stood.

"I will."

Grandma shook her head.

"No."

Temba frowned.

"Why?"

Grandma walked toward him.

She placed a small wooden charm in his hand.

"Your mother gave me this the night you were born."

Temba looked at it.

There was a tiny symbol carved into the wood.

Two circles joined by a line.

"What does it mean?"

Grandma answered softly.

"Two lives. One blood."

Temba closed his fingers around it.

They began walking.

The path to the hill was long.

No one spoke.

The farther they went, the colder the air became.

Birds flew away from the trees.

Even the grass looked dry.

Halfway up the hill, Chinwe stopped.

"Do you hear that?"

Temba listened.

A soft humming came from somewhere ahead.

A woman's voice.

Temba knew the song.

His mother used to sing it when he was a child.

He ran toward the sound.

Chinwe followed.

They reached a large tree at the top of the hill.

Someone was sitting beneath it.

A woman.

Her back was turned.

She was humming the same song.

Temba stopped.

His heart knew her before his eyes did.

"Mother?"

The woman stopped humming.

Slowly, she turned.

Temba could not breathe.

It was her.

Older.

Thinner.

But it was his mother.

She stood.

Tears ran down her face.

"Temba."

He ran toward her.

They held each other.

For a moment, everything felt normal.

Then Temba heard chains.

He pulled away.

His mother looked afraid.

"You came too early."

Temba stared at her.

"Too early for what?"

She looked toward the trees.

"Ikenna."

Temba's face tightened.

"Where is he?"

His mother pointed toward a large stone behind the tree.

A small red shirt was hanging from it.

Temba moved closer.

His mother grabbed his arm.

"Don't touch it."

Temba stopped.

"Why?"

"Because that is not a shirt."

The cloth moved.

It slowly lifted from the stone.

Something underneath it began to breathe.

Temba stepped back.

The red cloth fell to the ground.

A small hand appeared.

Then another.

Ikenna crawled from beneath the stone.

His face looked tired.

His eyes were no longer black.

He looked like a normal child.

Temba wanted to go to him.

His mother stopped him.

"Wait."

Ikenna looked at Temba.

"Brother."

Temba stepped forward.

"Ikenna."

Ikenna smiled.

"You finally came."

Then he looked at their mother.

"Tell him."

Their mother looked down.

Temba frowned.

"Tell me what?"

She took a deep breath.

"Ikenna was not taken by the ancestors."

Temba looked at him.

"Then who took him?"

His mother looked at the ground.

"I did."

Temba froze.

Chinwe covered her mouth.

Even Ikenna looked away.

"You?"

His mother nodded.

"I was the one who opened the door."

Temba felt anger rise inside him.

"Why?"

His mother looked at both boys.

"Because one of you had to be given to the ancestors."

Temba stepped back.

"Given?"

She cried.

"I had no choice."

"Everyone has a choice."

His mother looked at him.

"Not when the life of the whole village is in your hands."

The ground suddenly shook.

Ikenna grabbed Temba's hand.

"They are coming."

Temba looked around.

Dark figures appeared between the trees.

Dozens of them.

The children.

The ancestors.

And something much taller behind them.

His mother grabbed both boys.

"Listen to me."

She looked directly into Temba's eyes.

"The promise was never about Ikenna."

Temba's heart sank.

"Then who was it about?"

His mother pointed at him.

"You."

A deep voice came from the forest.

"Bring him."

The trees shook.

Ikenna squeezed Temba's hand.

"Run."

They turned to escape.

But the path behind them was gone.

Only a deep black hole remained.

Something moved inside it.

Then a hand reached out.

It grabbed their mother's ankle.

She screamed.

Temba grabbed her.

Ikenna grabbed Temba.

They pulled together.

The hand tightened.

Their mother looked at Temba.

"If I let go, don't come after me."

"No!"

She smiled through her tears.

"Protect your brother."

The hand pulled harder.

Their mother disappeared into the darkness.

Temba screamed.

Ikenna pulled him back.

The black hole closed.

Silence.

Then someone whispered behind them.

"Now there is only one thing left to choose."

Temba slowly turned.

A tall figure stood behind the tree.

Its face was hidden.

But it held something in its hand.

Grandma's red thread.

And attached to the thread was a small piece of white cloth.

Temba looked at Ikenna.

Ikenna was staring at the cloth.

His face had gone pale.

"Brother..."

"What?"

Ikenna whispered:

"That cloth is for the one who dies."

🌙 End of Chapter Five

🌗 Chapter Six – Cloth for the One Who Dies

Temba stared at the white cloth.

His fingers felt cold.

The tall figure stood beside the tree without moving.

Ikenna held Temba's hand tightly.

"Don't let him touch you."

Temba looked at his brother.

"Who is he?"

Ikenna shook his head.

"I don't know his name."

The figure lifted the white cloth.

A red thread was tied around it.

The thread stretched from the cloth to the ground.

Then it moved.

It crawled across the grass toward Temba.

Temba stepped back.

The thread followed.

Ikenna pulled him away.

"Run!"

They ran down the hill.

Behind them, the tall figure did not chase.

It simply watched.

That made Temba even more afraid.

They reached the trees.

The path twisted between them.

Ikenna knew where to go.

He pulled Temba through a narrow opening.

They ducked beneath a low branch.

Then they hid behind a large fallen tree.

Temba was breathing hard.

"Why didn't it follow us?"

Ikenna looked toward the hill.

"Because it doesn't need to."

Temba frowned.

"What does that mean?"

Ikenna looked at his wrist.

The red mark was glowing.

"It already knows where you are."

Temba pulled down his sleeve.

The mark was burning.

He rubbed it.

It became brighter.

Then he heard a sound behind them.

A soft whistle.

Three short notes.

Ikenna's face changed.

"We have to move."

They ran again.

After a while, they reached a small stream.

The water was clear.

Temba bent down to drink.

Ikenna stopped him.

"Don't."

Temba looked at him.

"Why?"

Ikenna pointed at the water.

There was a reflection.

Temba saw himself.

Then he saw Ikenna.

But there was someone standing behind them.

Their mother.

Temba turned quickly.

Nobody was there.

He looked back at the water.

His mother's reflection was still there.

She lifted one finger to her lips.

Then she pointed toward the other side of the stream.

Temba whispered:

"Mother?"

Ikenna grabbed his arm.

"Don't talk to the water."

The reflection of their mother suddenly smiled.

Her mouth opened.

But no sound came out.

Then the water turned black.

Temba stepped away.

A hand rose from the stream.

It grabbed the red thread around his wrist.

Temba screamed.

Ikenna pulled him back.

The hand disappeared.

The stream became clear again.

But something was floating on the surface.

A small wooden charm.

Temba picked it up.

It looked exactly like the one Grandma had given him.

He turned it over.

There was writing on the back.

WHEN ONE RETURNS, ONE MUST STAY.

Temba stared at the words.

"What does this mean?"

Ikenna looked away.

"You know what it means."

Temba shook his head.

"No, I don't."

Ikenna looked at him.

"One of us has to go back."

Temba felt his heart sink.

"Back to the shrine?"

Ikenna nodded.

"Yes."

Temba looked at him.

"You've been there for twenty years. Why can't you go back?"

Ikenna's face became sad.

"Because I already tried."

Temba waited.

"Three times."

"And each time they brought me back."

Temba looked at the forest.

"Why?"

Ikenna lowered his voice.

"Because I am not the one they want."

Temba's heart began to beat faster.

"Then who do they want?"

Ikenna looked directly at him.

"You."

A dry branch snapped behind them.

Both boys turned.

Nothing.

Another branch snapped.

Closer.

Ikenna pulled Temba toward the stream.

"Cross it."

They ran through the water.

Temba slipped.

Ikenna caught him.

They climbed the other side.

The forest behind them went silent.

Too silent.

Then a voice came from the trees.

"Ikenna."

Ikenna froze.

Temba looked at him.

"Who is calling you?"

Ikenna did not answer.

The voice came again.

"Ikenna."

This time, it sounded like their mother.

Ikenna closed his eyes.

"Don't listen."

The voice became softer.

"My son."

Ikenna's eyes filled with tears.

Temba held his hand.

"Come on."

They moved away.

Then the voice changed.

"Do you know what your brother did?"

Temba stopped.

Ikenna pulled him.

"Don't."

The voice continued.

"He knows why you were chosen."

Temba looked at Ikenna.

Ikenna could not meet his eyes.

"What did you do?"

Ikenna stayed silent.

"Ikenna, what did you do?"

The boy finally spoke.

"I tried to save you."

Temba stared at him.

"Save me from what?"

Ikenna looked toward the hill.

"From being taken."

He took a slow breath.

"When we were born, the elders were told that one of us would belong to the ancestors."

"Your mother refused."

"She tried to hide us."

Temba listened.

"But they found us."

Ikenna's voice shook.

"I was scared."

"So I told them they could take me."

Temba's eyes filled with tears.

"You chose yourself?"

Ikenna shook his head.

"No."

"I chose you."

Temba looked at him.

Ikenna continued.

"I thought they would let you live."

"But they made a mistake."

"They took the wrong boy."

Temba stared at him.

"What do you mean?"

Ikenna looked at Temba's wrist.

"They were supposed to take me."

"But the mark was already on you."

Temba touched his wrist.

The red mark was glowing again.

Then the ground beneath them shook.

The wooden charm fell from Temba's hand.

A crack appeared in the earth.

Red thread came out of it.

It wrapped around Ikenna's ankle.

Ikenna screamed.

Temba grabbed him.

"Hold on!"

The thread pulled harder.

Ikenna fell.

Temba held his arm.

The earth opened wider.

Darkness waited below.

Ikenna looked at Temba.

"Let me go."

"No!"

"If you hold me, they will take you too."

Temba refused to release him.

Then another hand came from the crack.

It grabbed Ikenna's other leg.

Temba screamed.

"Help!"

Nobody answered.

Then a woman's voice came from behind him.

"Temba."

He turned.

His mother stood across the stream.

She was alive.

But her eyes were full of fear.

"Let him go."

Temba shook his head.

"No."

His mother cried.

"If you don't, they will take both of you."

Temba looked down at Ikenna.

Ikenna was already disappearing into the darkness.

He reached up.

"Brother..."

Temba held tighter.

"I won't leave you."

Ikenna smiled.

"Then don't."

Suddenly, the red thread snapped.

Temba fell backward.

Ikenna vanished into the earth.

The crack closed.

Temba screamed his brother's name.

No answer came.

His mother ran across the stream.

She knelt beside him.

Temba grabbed her.

"You knew this would happen."

She cried.

"Yes."

"You knew they would take him again."

She nodded.

"Why did you bring me here?"

His mother looked at the closed ground.

"Because there is one thing I never told you."

Temba waited.

She reached into her dress.

She brought out a small black key.

"Ikenna is not trapped under the hill."

Temba stared at her.

"Then where is he?"

His mother pointed toward the village.

"Under our house."

Temba's blood ran cold.

Then the black key in his mother's hand began to shake.

A voice came from inside it.

"Brother..."

Temba leaned closer.

Ikenna whispered from inside the key.

"Don't trust Mother."
