🕵️ The Compound Thief

🌍 African Folktales • 🕒 30 min read

🍚 Chapter One – Who Ate My Rice and Beans?

“Who ate my rice and beans?”

Mimi's voice filled the compound.

“Mimi, why are you shouting?” someone asked.

“Who ate my rice and beans?”

“What happened?”

“Somebody ate my food.”

“What food?”

“Rice and beans.”

BC looked at her and shook her head.

“So you have rice and beans and lied to me that you did not cook?”

“Did you eat it?” Mimi asked.

“No.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes.”

“Are you sure? Because you're the only one who can smell food through concrete.”

“BC, enough!”

“BC, what is that supposed to mean?” Mimi asked.

BC folded her arms.

“See, finish. The day I would change it for you in this compound, it's because of your stinginess that someone ate your food.”

Mimi stared at her.

“It's just food. Relax. It's not national security.”

The argument continued for almost fifteen minutes.

Nobody confessed.

Nobody knew who had taken the food.

But somehow, Emeka already looked guilty.

That boy had never needed evidence against him. His own mouth did the job for free.

He was always around food. If somebody opened a pot, Emeka would somehow appear. If somebody entered the kitchen, Emeka would suddenly have a reason to pass there.

But this time, even Emeka denied eating Mimi's food.

“I didn't touch your rice,” he said.

“Then why are you standing here?” Mimi asked.

“Because I heard shouting.”

“And you came because you care?”

“Yes.”

BC laughed.

“You came because you thought there was food.”

Everybody laughed except Emeka.

That evening, the compound slowly became quiet again.

It was the kind of compound where everybody knew everybody's business, whether they asked for it or not.

Mama Nkechi, the landlady, owned the compound and kept all the keys. She had inherited the property from her late husband and ran the place with an iron fist wrapped in motherly affection.

Mimi was the eldest tenant among them and was usually the first person to discover anything strange.

Bisi, whom everyone called BC, was the busybody of the compound. She loved crime documentaries and believed she was an investigator.

Emeka loved food more than anything else.

And then there was Shade.

Shade was quiet and reserved, but her tongue was sharp enough to slice pepper when she decided to use it.

At first, nobody thought much about Mimi's missing food.

People lose things sometimes.

Food disappears.

Someone forgets where they kept something.

Life continues.

But the next morning, our compound woke up to a completely different kind of wahala.

“Who is inside now?” somebody shouted near the shared bathroom.

“Please hurry up and come outside.”

Another voice followed.

“Are you doing renovations inside?”

People started gathering.

“How long does one need to bathe?”

Someone laughed.

“Maybe they are washing their sins.”

After several minutes, the bathroom door finally opened.

Shade stepped out.

She looked at everyone standing there.

“Why is there so much noise here?”

“If you wanted to use the bathroom, you should have come a bit earlier,” someone said.

Shade ignored the comment and walked away.

Then Emeka suddenly looked around.

“Has anybody seen my soap?”

Shade stopped.

“What?”

“My soap. I left it here yesterday.”

Shade looked at him.

“No, I did not see your soap. Can I go now?”

“Okay.”

She walked away.

At first, the missing soap seemed like nothing.

But later that day, somebody noticed that toothpaste was missing.

Then cooking oil.

Then detergent.

Then one of the phone chargers disappeared.

Then somebody could not find a pair of slippers.

Everyone started looking at everyone.

The compound that was once full of laughter suddenly became quiet.

People started locking their doors more carefully.

Food was no longer left in the shared fridge without someone checking it twice.

The shared fridge had become the most dangerous appliance in the compound.

And the worst part was that nobody knew who was doing it.

Then one evening, Mama Nkechi opened the fridge and screamed.

“My jollof rice!”

Everyone came outside.

Mimi looked at her.

“What happened?”

“Somebody has eaten my jollof rice.”

BC slowly folded her arms.

“Hmm.”

Everybody looked at her.

“What?” she asked.

“Why did you say hmm?” Mimi asked.

“Because this is no longer coincidence.”

BC looked around the compound.

“We have a thief.”

The compound went silent.

For the first time, everybody understood that Mimi's missing rice and beans had not been an isolated incident.

Someone was stealing from them.

And whoever it was had been watching them closely.

That night, nobody slept comfortably.

And BC made a decision.

She was going to find the thief herself.

🌙 End of Chapter One

🔎 Chapter Two – The Compound Detective

By the next morning, BC had already turned her room into an investigation headquarters.

She had a notebook on the table, a pen in her hand, and a serious look on her face.

On the first page, she had written in big letters:

“THE COMPOUND THIEF.”

Under it, she had written a list of everything that had disappeared.

Rice and beans.

Jollof rice.

Chicken stew.

Soap.

Toothpaste.

Cooking oil.

Detergent.

Chargers.

Slippers.

BC stared at the list and nodded slowly.

“This person is not hungry alone,” she said. “This person has a shopping list.”

Mimi looked at her.

“BC, are you serious?”

“Very serious.”

“You are investigating soap now?”

“A thief is a thief. Today it is soap. Tomorrow it may be your television.”

Mama Nkechi came outside.

“What is happening here?”

BC stood up.

“Mama, we have to be careful.”

“Careful about what?”

“The thief.”

Mama Nkechi frowned.

“Which thief?”

BC pointed at her notebook.

“The person stealing from this compound.”

Mama Nkechi looked at the list.

“And you know who it is?”

“Not yet.”

“Then why are you disturbing everybody?”

“Because the person is among us.”

The compound became quiet.

Everyone looked at one another.

BC decided to question everybody.

She started with Mimi.

“Where were you yesterday evening?”

Mimi looked at her.

“In my room.”

“Doing what?”

“Sleeping.”

“For how long?”

“BC, are you a police officer?”

“Just answer the question.”

Mimi rolled her eyes.

“I was sleeping.”

BC wrote something down.

“Interesting.”

“What did you write?”

“I cannot reveal that.”

“You are the one investigating us!”

BC ignored her.

Next was Emeka.

“Where were you yesterday night?”

“My room.”

“Did you enter the kitchen?”

“No.”

“Did you open the fridge?”

“No.”

“Did you eat anything?”

Emeka paused.

“What kind of question is that?”

BC narrowed her eyes.

“Answer me.”

“I ate my own food.”

“What did you eat?”

“Rice.”

“Whose rice?”

Emeka looked offended.

“My own rice.”

BC wrote something in her notebook.

Emeka looked at her suspiciously.

“Why are you writing?”

“Nothing.”

“Let me see.”

“No.”

“Then stop writing about me.”

BC moved on.

She questioned Shade.

She questioned Mama Nkechi.

She even questioned herself in the mirror.

By afternoon, everybody was tired of her investigation.

But BC was not giving up.

That evening, people started locking their food inside their rooms.

The shared fridge was almost empty.

Nobody trusted anybody anymore.

Even when someone entered the kitchen to get water, three people would look through their windows.

The compound had changed.

There was no longer the usual laughter.

There was suspicion everywhere.

Then, around midnight, BC heard a sound.

Clink.

She opened her eyes.

Another sound came from the kitchen.

Clink.

BC quietly got out of bed.

She opened her door slowly.

She moved through the passage like someone in a crime movie.

When she reached the kitchen, she saw someone standing there.

BC quickly switched on the light.

“Aha!”

Emeka jumped.

“Jesus!”

“What are you doing here?” BC asked.

Emeka held up a cup.

“I came to get water.”

BC stared at him.

“At midnight?”

“Am I not allowed to be thirsty at midnight?”

BC looked around.

“Did you open the fridge?”

“No.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes.”

“Then why are you standing beside it?”

Emeka sighed.

“Because the water is here.”

BC stared at him for a few seconds.

“Go to your room.”

“Gladly.”

Emeka walked away.

BC remained in the kitchen.

She checked the fridge.

Nothing seemed missing.

But then she noticed something.

There was a faint smell of red stew around the fridge handle.

BC touched the handle and smelled her fingers.

Her eyes widened.

“Hmm.”

She smiled.

“Got you.”

The thief was not only taking food.

The thief was eating it right there.

BC immediately called Mimi, Mama Nkechi, Shade and Emeka together the next morning.

“I have an idea,” she said.

“What idea?” Mimi asked.

BC leaned closer.

“We are going to set a trap.”

Emeka's eyes opened wide.

“What kind of trap?”

BC smiled.

“A food trap.”

Emeka immediately became interested.

“What food?”

Everyone looked at him.

“What?” he asked.

BC shook her head.

“You are exactly why this investigation is difficult.”

That evening, they prepared the trap.

They made a fresh plate of jollof rice and placed a big piece of chicken beside it.

The smell alone could make a fasting person reconsider their entire relationship with religion.

But there was something special about the food.

BC had secretly put a little blue colouring on the chicken and around the container.

Anyone who touched it would have blue stains on their fingers.

Then they placed the food inside the shared fridge.

After that, the five of them hid.

They waited.

Ten o'clock passed.

Eleven o'clock passed.

Midnight came.

Still nothing.

Emeka whispered, “Maybe the thief is not coming.”

Mimi turned and looked at him.

“Keep quiet.”

“But I'm hungry.”

“You were not invited to eat the trap.”

“I know. I'm just saying.”

One o'clock in the morning came.

Then suddenly, they heard it.

Creeeeak.

The kitchen door slowly opened.

Everyone held their breath.

A shadow entered the kitchen.

The person looked around carefully.

Then the fridge door opened.

The compound thief had finally arrived.

🌿 Moral of the Story

Sometimes, people make bad choices when they are facing difficult situations. But hiding the truth and taking what belongs to others can create even bigger problems. It is always better to speak up, ask for help, and protect the trust people have placed in you.
