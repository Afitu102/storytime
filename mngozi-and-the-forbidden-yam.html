🍠 Mngozi and the Forbidden Yam

🌍 African Folktales • 🕒 30 min read

😏 Chapter One – The Woman Who Hated Instructions

There was one thing everyone in the community knew about Mngozi: if you told her that she could not do something, you had just made that thing the most interesting thing in the world to her.

Mngozi did not like being told what to do. She believed that people should be free to make their own choices, and nobody had the right to decide what she could or could not do.

Even when the matter was something small, Mngozi would find a way to challenge it.

One day, she went to buy a pair of shoes. The seller carefully arranged the shoes on a table and warned her not to touch one particular pair.

“Please, don't touch that one,” the seller said.

Mngozi looked at the shoe.

Then she looked at the seller.

“Why?” she asked.

“Just don't touch it.”

That was enough to make Mngozi curious.

If the woman had simply left the shoe there without saying anything, Mngozi probably would not have cared. But now that she had been told not to touch it, she wanted to know why.

That was how Mngozi was.

Her husband, Emeka, knew this better than anyone.

Whenever they had an argument, Emeka sometimes tried to end the conversation quickly. But Mngozi always wanted the last word.

If Emeka said ten words, Mngozi would find twenty words to answer him.

“Mngozi, please, let this matter rest,” Emeka would say.

“Why should I let it rest when you are still talking?” she would reply.

It was almost impossible for anyone to give Mngozi an instruction without getting an argument in return.

For Mngozi, freedom meant doing things at her own time and in her own way.

She enjoyed being able to order food whenever she wanted without worrying about preparing a meal in a kitchen full of family members. She liked waking up when she felt like it, doing her chores at her own pace, and spending her day without someone constantly reminding her about rules.

That was one of the reasons she had avoided visiting her husband's village for three years.

Three years earlier, Mngozi had married Emeka in a traditional wedding. Since then, Emeka had travelled home several times for important family occasions.

Every time he packed his clothes and prepared to leave, he would ask her to come with him.

And every time, Mngozi had the same answer.

“You can go. I will stay here.”

Sometimes she would sit comfortably on the sofa and watch Nollywood movies while Emeka prepared for his journey.

Emeka could not understand why his wife refused to visit his family.

It was not because his family had offended her. It was not because they had treated her badly.

Mngozi simply did not like the idea of village family gatherings, where there were always rules, traditions, instructions, and people telling her what was expected of her.

But this time, things were different.

Emeka's mother had become determined to see her daughter-in-law at the August meeting.

People in the village had started asking questions.

“Why does Emeka's wife never come home?”

“Is she proud?”

“Does she dislike the family?”

“Maybe she does not want anything to do with the village.”

The truth was much simpler.

Mngozi just wanted to live without anyone telling her what she should do.

But Emeka's mother refused to give up.

When Emeka told Mngozi that his mother wanted all the daughters-in-law to attend the August meeting, Mngozi sighed.

“All the daughters-in-law?” she asked.

“Yes,” Emeka replied.

“And she really wants me to come?”

“Very much.”

Mngozi sat quietly for a moment.

Then an idea came to her.

If they wanted her there so badly, she would go.

But she was not going to behave the way they expected.

She would misbehave enough for everyone to regret inviting her.

In her mind, it was a brilliant plan.

“Fine,” she finally said. “I will go.”

Emeka looked surprised.

He did not know that Mngozi had already decided to make her first visit unforgettable.

On the morning of their departure, Mngozi packed her clothes carefully. She carried enough outfits for the journey, but in her mind, she was preparing for something much bigger than an ordinary family visit.

When they finally arrived at the village, Emeka's mother welcomed them warmly.

She hugged Mngozi with genuine happiness.

“My daughter, you finally came!” she said.

Mngozi smiled.

She looked around the village and quietly counted the days until she could put her plan into action.

She had no idea that the village would soon teach her a lesson she would never forget.

🌙 End of Chapter One

🧣 Chapter Two – Mngozi's Trouble at the August Meeting

When Mngozi arrived at the village with Emeka, his mother was delighted to see her.

For three years, she had heard stories about her son's wife but had rarely seen her. Now that Mngozi had finally agreed to attend the August meeting, she welcomed her with open arms.

Mngozi smiled and returned the greeting, but inside her mind, she was already thinking about the plan she had made.

She wanted everyone to see that bringing her to the village had been a mistake.

The August meeting was going to be her opportunity.

Before the meeting day arrived, Emeka took time to explain everything to her.

“Mngozi, please listen carefully,” he said. “Don't arrive late. Everyone is expected to be there on time. And make sure you wear the required uniform.”

Mngozi looked at him.

“What happens if someone doesn't wear the uniform?” she asked.

“They will pay a fine.”

“And what if the person refuses to pay the fine?”

Emeka looked at her suspiciously.

“Are you planning not to wear the uniform?”

Mngozi laughed.

“Me? No. I'm only asking.”

But Emeka knew his wife well enough to suspect that her questions were rarely just questions.

Finally, the day of the August meeting arrived.

While everyone else was getting ready, Mngozi took her time.

She moved slowly, choosing what to wear and taking her time with everything. The more people reminded her that they were already getting late, the slower she seemed to become.

“Mngozi, we should be going,” Emeka reminded her.

“I'm coming.”

A few minutes later, he reminded her again.

“I'm almost ready.”

By the time Mngozi finally left the house, the meeting had already started.

She arrived almost two hours late.

The moment she entered the meeting ground, people turned to look at her.

Mngozi did not appear embarrassed.

Instead, she walked in as though she had arrived exactly when she was supposed to.

Then one of the women noticed something else.

“Mngozi,” the woman called, “your scarf is the wrong colour.”

Mngozi looked at the scarf around her head.

“Oh, I didn't know that colour of scarf was compulsory.”

The woman explained that the colour was part of the meeting's rules.

Mngozi frowned.

“See me see trouble, oh. Is it by force to tie that particular type of scarf?”

The woman tried to explain that everyone was expected to follow the rules.

Mngozi continued asking questions.

Why was the scarf compulsory? Why did she have to pay a fine for arriving late? Why couldn't people simply come whenever they were ready?

After several minutes of back-and-forth, Mngozi eventually paid the fine.

But she did not do it quietly.

She complained loudly enough for everyone nearby to know that she had been fined.

Some women exchanged glances.

Others shook their heads.

This was Mngozi's first real introduction to many of the village women, and already they had an impression of her.

But Mngozi was not finished.

After the meeting, food was served.

There was melon soup, bitterleaf soup, beans, and other dishes prepared for everyone.

Mngozi looked at the food and forgot about trying to behave carefully.

She ate.

And she ate some more.

She ate as though she had not seen food in days. She ate quickly, chewed loudly, and paid little attention to the women sitting around her.

Some of the women became uncomfortable.

Others were embarrassed on her behalf.

By the time the meal was over, Mngozi had successfully achieved almost everything she wanted.

She had arrived late.

She had worn the wrong scarf.

She had argued over the fine.

And now she had given everyone another reason to talk about her.

As people left the meeting ground, Mngozi could hear the comments.

“That woman is very rude.”

“She doesn't know village etiquette.”

“How can somebody behave like that at an August meeting?”

Mngozi secretly smiled.

This was exactly what she wanted.

She thought that if she behaved badly enough, nobody would want her to return for the next celebration.

As far as she was concerned, her plan was working perfectly.

But there was one person who did not see things the same way.

Her mother-in-law.

Instead of being angry, the older woman was simply happy that all her daughters-in-law were finally together.

Later, she called Mngozi aside.

“My daughter, did you enjoy the meeting?” she asked.

Mngozi nodded.

She was exhausted and had no strength left to explain anything.

Her mother-in-law smiled and began telling her about another important celebration that was coming soon.

“The New Yam Festival will be here before you know it,” she said.

Mngozi looked up.

“New Yam Festival?”

Her mother-in-law explained that the new harvest had to be presented properly before people could begin eating the new yam.

It was a traditional ceremony where the elders performed rites and gave thanks for the harvest. Until the ceremony was completed, people were expected to wait.

Mngozi listened carefully.

Then she asked the question that would soon cause serious trouble.

“So nobody can eat the new yam before that?”

Her mother-in-law nodded.

“Yes, my daughter. That is the tradition.”

Mngozi smiled.

“Okay, nah. No problem. Good night, Mama.”

She walked away calmly.

But inside her mind, one thought had already taken root.

She had just been told that there was something she was not allowed to do.

And for Mngozi, that was never the end of the conversation.

It was the beginning of a challenge.

🌙 End of Chapter Two

🍠 Chapter Three – The Forbidden New Yam

From the moment Mngozi heard about the New Yam Festival, she could not stop thinking about the new yam.

Her mother-in-law had explained the tradition clearly. The new harvest had to be presented properly before anyone was expected to eat it. The elders would perform the traditional rites and give thanks for the harvest, and everyone was supposed to wait until the ceremony was completed.

For most people, that explanation was enough.

For Mngozi, it was a challenge.

She kept asking herself one question.

“What will really happen if I eat the new yam?”

There were old yams stored in the barn. There were also plantains, rice, beans, and other food in the house. Mngozi had plenty to eat.

But none of those things interested her anymore.

She wanted the new yam.

The more she remembered that she had been told not to eat it, the stronger her curiosity became.

“Maybe they are only saying all these things because they want people to obey the tradition,” she thought.

She convinced herself that there was nothing special about the yam.

It was simply a yam.

She just wanted to prove it for herself.

The opportunity came the following morning.

The entire household was busy preparing for a family member's marriage introduction. People were moving around the compound, getting things ready and making sure everything was in place.

Emeka was also busy and barely paid attention to what Mngozi was doing.

Mngozi noticed this.

It was the perfect opportunity.

She quietly made her way to the backyard farm.

She looked around carefully.

Nobody was watching.

Then she spotted it.

A beautiful new yam.

She picked it up and quickly returned to the house.

Her heart was beating fast.

She felt like a child who had secretly stolen a piece of meat from the kitchen.

She waited until everyone had left for the marriage introduction ceremony.

When the compound finally became quiet, Mngozi went into the kitchen.

She prepared the yam herself.

She added palm oil, chopped onions, pepper, and fresh uba.

The smell filled the kitchen.

When she finished cooking, she looked at the plate proudly.

“So this is the food everybody has been warning me about,” she said to herself.

She placed the plate where she could eat comfortably.

Then she climbed onto a small wooden chair so she could sit properly.

She took the first bite.

She stopped.

She looked around.

Nothing happened.

No thunder.

No strange voice.

No mysterious sound.

Everything was completely normal.

Mngozi took another bite.

Then another.

Before long, she had finished the food.

She smiled.

“So what was all that warning about?” she muttered.

She cleaned the kitchen carefully. She washed the plate, cleaned the cooking area, and made sure there was no evidence that she had taken the new yam.

She felt victorious.

To Mngozi, the whole tradition suddenly seemed unnecessary.

She checked herself several times during the day.

She looked at her hands.

She touched her face.

She walked around the compound.

Everything seemed normal.

“See?” she thought. “Nothing happened.”

She became so confident that she stopped thinking about the matter.

Later, she went to take a nap.

But only a few minutes after lying down, something strange began to happen.

A sharp pain started in her stomach.

Mngozi opened her eyes.

She sat up slowly and held her stomach.

“What is this?” she wondered.

The pain became stronger.

Her body suddenly felt uncomfortable, and she began to wonder if the yam was responsible.

She walked to the mirror.

Then she froze when she saw her reflection.

Her mouth was swollen.

It had become so large that she could hardly recognize herself.

“What happened to my face?” she whispered.

She touched her lips, wondering if an insect had bitten her.

But the swelling continued.

Her stomach made another painful sound.

Now she remembered the yam.

For the first time, Mngozi began to wonder if she had made a terrible mistake.

Still, she tried to convince herself that it was nothing serious.

“Maybe I will be fine tomorrow,” she said.

She did not want anyone to see her like that, so she went to bed early before the family returned.

She hoped that by morning, the swelling would disappear.

But when morning came, Mngozi discovered that her problem had only just begun.

🌙 End of Chapter Three

😨 Chapter Four – The Price of Disobedience

The next morning, Mngozi woke up hoping that the strange swelling on her face had disappeared.

But the moment she looked in the mirror, her heart sank.

The swelling was still there.

In fact, it looked worse.

Her lips were badly swollen, and her stomach was still troubling her. She tried to understand what was happening, but deep down, she already knew what had caused it.

The new yam.

Before she could decide what to do, Emeka entered the room with some members of the family.

They had come to check on her.

The moment they saw her face, everyone stopped and stared.

Emeka looked worried.

“Ngozi, what did you eat?” he asked.

Mngozi lowered her head.

Emeka looked at her carefully.

“Hope it's not what I'm thinking.”

For a few seconds, Mngozi remained silent.

There was no point hiding it anymore.

“I ate the new yam,” she finally admitted.

Everyone became quiet.

“You did what?” Emeka asked.

Mngozi explained that she had wanted to know what would happen if she ate it before the New Yam Festival.

She had heard the warning, but because nobody had been able to tell her exactly what would happen, her curiosity had taken over.

Her mother-in-law was deeply disappointed.

She reminded Mngozi that the tradition was not something people were supposed to treat carelessly.

According to the traditional belief of the community, the new yam was sacred until the proper ceremony had been performed. Breaking that custom was considered a serious matter.

Mngozi listened quietly.

For perhaps the first time, she did not argue.

She had no clever answer.

She had no question to ask.

She simply sat there with her swollen face and painful stomach, wondering why she had refused to listen.

The family made sure she received treatment, and the days that followed were uncomfortable for her.

She spent much of her time resting and recovering.

Then something else happened that made the whole village talk.

A few days later, unusually heavy rain came down over the area.

The rain was so strong that some of the crops that had been carefully prepared for the coming festival were damaged.

People became worried.

The elders gathered and discussed what had happened.

Many of them believed that the damage was connected to the violation of the traditional order.

They remembered that Mngozi had eaten the new yam before the proper ceremony.

To them, the timing was too close to ignore.

Whether the heavy rain was truly connected to what Mngozi had done was something nobody could prove for certain.

But that was what many people in the community believed at the time.

Mngozi heard the discussions.

For the first time, the situation no longer seemed like a simple challenge between herself and a village rule.

She began to understand that traditions were connected to the lives of many people, not just to one person.

She thought about the August meeting.

She remembered how she had arrived late, argued about the scarf, complained about the fine, and behaved as though every rule was an attack on her freedom.

Then she remembered the new yam.

She had been told clearly to wait.

Instead of asking questions and respecting the tradition, she had decided to prove that nobody could tell her what to do.

Now she was paying the price for that attitude.

Fortunately, her condition did not last forever.

With treatment and enough rest, the swelling gradually went down.

The pain in her stomach also disappeared.

But something inside Mngozi had changed.

She was no longer as eager to argue as she had been before.

When someone explained a rule to her, she listened.

When someone asked her to follow a family tradition, she no longer immediately demanded to know why she should obey.

She began to understand that listening did not mean losing her freedom.

It meant respecting the people around her.

And there was one thing she would never forget.

Whenever Mngozi saw a new yam after that, she avoided it as though it were dangerous.

If someone offered her yam before the proper time, she would politely decline.

“No, thank you,” she would say.

The woman who once could not resist anything she was told not to do had finally learned that some warnings were given for a reason.

But her family had not yet heard the most important words from her.

Mngozi still had one thing left to do.

🌙 End of Chapter Four
